Spec 14.1 – 14.2 Brønsted–Lowry

Proton transfer & conjugate pairs

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define: Brønsted–Lowry acid = proton (H⁺) donor; base = proton acceptor.
  • Describe acid–base reactions as proton-transfer processes.
  • Identify conjugate pairs: species differing by one H⁺ (HA/A⁻, NH₄⁺/NH₃, H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻).
  • Label both pairs in any equation, e.g. CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ (water acts as a base here).
  • Know water is amphoteric — it can donate or accept a proton depending on its partner.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Say “proton”, never “hydrogen atom” or “H⁺ ion accepts an electron”. Conjugate pairs must differ by exactly one H⁺ and opposite charge — H₂SO₄/SO₄²⁻ is NOT a conjugate pair.

Spec 14.3 – 14.5 pH defined

pH — definition and both directions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define $pH = -\log_{10}[H^+]$ (hydrogen ion concentration in mol dm⁻³).
  • Calculate pH from [H⁺] with the calculator’s log key.
  • Reverse it: $[H^+] = 10^{-pH}$ using 10^x / INV log.
  • Keep significant figures sensible: pH to 2 d.p. is the exam norm.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

[H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ is a guaranteed 1-marker (14.5) — practise the key-in until automatic. pH 2.40 → 3.98 × 10⁻³, not 2.4 × 10⁻¹.

Spec 14.6 – 14.7 Strong vs weak

Degree of dissociation & pH of strong acids

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Strong acid: fully dissociated (~100%) in aqueous solution — HCl, HNO₃, H₂SO₄.
  • Weak acid: partially dissociated (~1%) — equilibrium lies far left: CH₃COOH ⇌ H⁺ + CH₃COO⁻.
  • Same concentration ≠ same pH: 0.1 M HCl is pH 1.0; 0.1 M CH₃COOH is pH 2.87.
  • Strong monoprotic: [H⁺] = c → pH = −log c. For H₂SO₄ in this syllabus: [H⁺] = 2c.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Concentrated” ≠ “strong” — strength is degree of dissociation; concentration is mol dm⁻³. A dilute strong acid can have a higher pH than a concentrated weak one. The ×2 for H₂SO₄ is a favourite trap.

Spec 14.8 – 14.9 Ka & weak-acid pH

The acid dissociation constant and its two assumptions

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Deduce $K_a = \dfrac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$ from the dissociation equation; units mol dm⁻³.
  • Apply the two assumptions: [H⁺] = [A⁻] and [HA]eq ≈ initial [HA].
  • Hence $[H^+] = \sqrt{K_a \cdot c}$ and pH — no quadratic equations are ever required.
  • Know $pK_a = -\log K_a$ and $K_a = 10^{-pK_a}$; smaller pKa = stronger acid.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

State the assumptions when the question asks — they are mark points, not decoration. Shortcut pH = ½(pKa − log c) is fine in working, but be ready to show where it comes from. The three weak-acid assumptions: (1) [HA]eq ≈ [HA]initial, (2) [H⁺] = [A⁻] (ignore H⁺ from water), (3) second dissociation negligible — not bare “dissociation is partial”. Marking chain: M1 x² = Ka×[HA], M2 [H⁺] = √(…) to 3 s.f. (TE on M2 only if pH < 7), M3 pH = −log[H⁺]. pH = −log Ka is a classic 2-mark loss (pKa ≠ pH). Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q3 — treat CH₃COOH as weak, [H⁺]²/Ka, not strong.

Spec 14.10 – 14.12 Kw & strong bases

The ionic product of water, pKw, and base pH

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define $K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K; $pK_w = 14$.
  • Strong base route: [OH⁻] = c → pOH = −log[OH⁻] → pH = 14 − pOH.
  • Define pKa = −log Ka and pKw = −log Kw; convert both ways.
  • Neutral means [H⁺] = [OH⁻] — not pH 7 at every temperature (Kw rises with T because dissociation of water is endothermic, so pure water at 373 K has pH ≈ 6.1 yet is still neutral).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Putting [OH⁻] straight into −log gives pOH, not pH — the most common error on strong-base questions. Golden procedure (Paper 4 digest): [OH⁻] → pOH → pH = 14 − pOH. Diprotic base: Ba(OH)₂ gives 2 OH⁻ — compute pOH from 2×[Ba(OH)₂] first (WCH14 Jun 2026 Q4: pH = 13.18). Total-volume trap: when acid meets base and one is in excess, divide [OH⁻] (or [H⁺]) by the total mixed volume — dividing by acid-only/base-only/volume-difference all lose a mark.

Spec 14.13 – 14.14 Data & dilution

Analysing pH data — dilution fingerprints and Ka from experiment

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Compare equimolar strong/weak acids and bases by pH (strong acid pH far lower at same c).
  • Dilution fingerprint: ×10 dilution → strong acid pH +1; weak acid pH +0.5 (because [H⁺] = √(Kac)).
  • Explain salt solutions: NH₄Cl acidic (NH₄⁺ donates H⁺ to water), CH₃COONa alkaline (CH₃COO⁻ accepts H⁺).
  • Calculate Ka from data (14.14): mass → c, measured pH → [H⁺], then Ka = [H⁺]²/c.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The +1 vs +0.5 dilution rule is a recurring exam discriminator — learn it with the reason (√ dependence), not as a bare fact. Watch the live version in Engine B.

Spec 14.15 Titration curves

Drawing & interpreting all four curve shapes

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Sketch pH vs volume for: strong/strong (eq pH 7), weak acid/strong base (eq ≈ 8.7), strong acid/weak base (eq ≈ 5.3), weak/weak (no jump).
  • Explain the shapes: weak-acid curves start higher, pass through a buffer region, and jump where the salt hydrolysis sets the equivalence pH.
  • Extend to diprotic acids: two dissociation steps → two equivalence points for a weak diprotic acid (e.g. ethanedioic); H₂SO₄ behaves as fully dissociated.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Sketches need: labelled axes, correct starting pH, the vertical jump at the right volume, and the correct equivalence pH. Jan 2026 MS rewards the alkaline endpoint of weak-acid/strong-base curves explicitly.

Spec 14.16 Indicators

Choosing the right indicator from the curve

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Rule: the indicator’s colour-change range must lie within the vertical pH jump of the curve.
  • Phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0 (colourless ⇄ pink) → weak acid/strong base and strong/strong.
  • Methyl orange 3.1–4.4 (red ⇄ yellow) → strong acid/weak base and strong/strong.
  • Weak/weak: no sharp jump → no suitable visual indicator; use a pH meter.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Paper 4 staple (Jan 2024 Q6, Q19ciii): methyl orange for strong acid/weak base, phenolphthalein for weak acid/strong base — with the reason “range lies within the steep section”. Test it live in the Titration Lab. WCH14 Jun 2026 Q8a: phenol red for weak acid/strong base (bromophenol blue / bromocresol green / methyl orange are wrong range). Equivalence pH ≠ 7: weak acid + strong base → pH > 7 because the conjugate base reacts with water (CH₃CH₂COO⁻ + H₂O → CH₃CH₂COOH + OH⁻) — give the equation for all 3 marks.

Spec 14.17 – 14.18 Buffers defined

What a buffer is and exactly how it works

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define: a solution that resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added.
  • Composition: weak acid + its conjugate base (salt), e.g. CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa — or weak base + its salt (NH₃/NH₄Cl).
  • Mechanism: added H⁺ is removed by A⁻ (→ HA); added OH⁻ is removed by HA (→ A⁻ + H₂O). Write both equations.
  • Explain why pH barely moves: the [A⁻]/[HA] ratio changes only slightly, and pH depends on that ratio.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Keeps the pH constant” loses the mark — it resists/minimises change. Strong acid + its salt is NOT a buffer. Both removal equations must appear for full mechanism marks. Build and stress one in Engine C.

Spec 14.19 – 14.21 Buffer maths & curves

Buffer pH, making buffers, and the half-equivalence trick

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Calculate buffer pH: $[H^+] = K_a\dfrac{[HA]}{[A^-]}$ or pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]); moles may replace concentrations (common volume).
  • Prepare a buffer of given pH: find the required ratio from the equation, then masses/volumes (Jan 2024 Q17biii style).
  • Two preparation routes: mix weak acid + its salt directly, or partially neutralise the weak acid with strong base (salt forms in situ).
  • On a weak-acid/strong-base curve: the flat region before equivalence IS buffer action; at half-neutralisation pH = pKa → read Ka straight off the graph (14.21).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

pH = pKa at half-equivalence is one of the highest-frequency one-liners in Paper 4 (Jan 2024 Q19ci). In preparation questions, show the ratio step before converting to mass. Buffer-mechanism 3-marker (acidic buffer + OH⁻): (1) large reservoir of CH₃COOH present, (2) CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O removes OH⁻, (3) [acid]:[salt] ratio stays almost unchanged → pH almost constant. REJECT “pH stays constant”. Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q6 — 0.2 mol CH₃COOH + 0.1 mol Mg(OH)₂ makes a buffer; Q7 — buffer pH needs [CH₃CH₂COOH] > [CH₃CH₂COO⁻], not pH = pKa.

Spec 14.22 – 14.23 Buffers in life · CP11

Biological buffers & Core Practical 11

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Blood buffer: H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ maintains pH ≈ 7.40. Added acid: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃ (→ CO₂ + H₂O, exhaled). Added alkali: H₂CO₃ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O.
  • Enzymes denature outside a narrow pH range — why cells and blood must be buffered.
  • Food buffers: resist pH changes caused by bacterial/fungal activity, preventing deterioration.
  • CP11 — find Ka of a weak acid: measure the pH of a solution of known concentration (or use the half-neutralisation method).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Blood questions want the two equations AND the pH 7.4 fact. Note the blood ratio [HCO₃⁻]/[H₂CO₃] ≈ 20:1 — set the Buffer Builder to the blood preset and check it gives 7.4.

Out of scope in this studio: pH calculations for weak bases (Kb numerics — the syllabus requires strong bases only), salt-hydrolysis calculations (qualitative only via 14.13), activity effects, and stepwise Ka arithmetic for polyprotic acids beyond curve interpretation. Topic 13 (Kc) is treated separately.

pH Calculation Procedures — every situation, step by step

6 situations · shortcuts included
1. Strong acid → pH (14.7)
1
Strong acids dissociate completely, so $[H^+] = c$ (×2 for H₂SO₄ in this syllabus).
2
$pH = -\log_{10}[H^+]$.
Worked: 0.050 M HCl → [H⁺] = 0.050 → pH = −log(0.050) = 1.30.  0.050 M H₂SO₄ → [H⁺] = 0.10 → pH = 1.00.
⚡ Shortcut: pH = −log c, one line. The only trap is the diprotic ×2.
2. Strong base → pH (14.11)
1
NaOH → Na⁺ + OH⁻ complete, so $[OH^-] = c$.
2
$pOH = -\log_{10}[OH^-]$.
3
$pH = 14 - pOH$ (since $pH + pOH = pK_w = 14$ at 298 K).
Worked: 0.020 M NaOH → pOH = −log(0.020) = 1.70 → pH = 14 − 1.70 = 12.30.
⚡ Shortcut: pH = 14 + log c. Never −log the hydroxide and call it pH.
3. Weak acid → pH (14.9)
1
Write $HA \rightleftharpoons H^+ + A^-$ with $K_a = \dfrac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$.
2
Two assumptions: [H⁺] = [A⁻] (1:1 dissociation, ignore water’s H⁺) and [HA]eq ≈ c (dissociation is tiny).
3
$K_a = [H^+]^2 / c$ → $[H^+] = \sqrt{K_a \cdot c}$.
4
$pH = -\log[H^+]$.
Worked: 0.10 M CH₃COOH, Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ → [H⁺] = √(1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.10) = 1.34 × 10⁻³ → pH = 2.87.
⚡ Shortcut: pH = ½(pKa − log c) = ½(4.74 − log 0.10) = ½(5.74) = 2.87. No quadratics — the assumptions are the whole method.
4. Buffer → pH (14.19)
1
Rearrange Ka: $[H^+] = K_a \times \dfrac{[HA]}{[A^-]}$.
2
Insert concentrations (or moles — the shared volume cancels in the ratio).
3
pH = −log[H⁺].
Worked: 0.20 M CH₃COOH + 0.10 M CH₃COONa → [H⁺] = 1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.20/0.10 = 3.6×10⁻⁵ → pH = 4.44.
⚡ Shortcut: Henderson–Hasselbalch: pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]). Equal concentrations → pH = pKa.
5. pH → [H⁺] (14.5)
1
$[H^+] = 10^{-pH}$ — calculator: 10^x key, enter −pH.
Worked: pH 2.40 → [H⁺] = 10⁻²·⁴⁰ = 3.98 × 10⁻³ mol dm⁻³. pH 11.30 → 5.01 × 10⁻¹².
⚡ Shortcut: it IS the shortcut — but check the mantissa: pH 2.40 is NOT 2.4 × 10⁻¹.
6. Kw, pKw and the temperature twist (14.10–14.12)
1
$K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 1.0 \times 10^{-14}$ mol² dm⁻⁶ at 298 K; $pK_w = -\log K_w = 14$.
2
Pure water: [H⁺] = [OH⁻] = √Kw = 10⁻⁷ → pH 7 at 298 K.
3
Water’s dissociation is endothermic, so heating raises Kw. At 373 K, Kw ≈ 5.5 × 10⁻¹³ → [H⁺] = √Kw ≈ 7.4 × 10⁻⁷ → pH ≈ 6.1 — still neutral because [H⁺] = [OH⁻].
⚡ Exam line: neutrality is defined by [H⁺] = [OH⁻], not by pH 7. “Hot water is acidic” is the trap — it isn’t.

Weak Acids, Ka & the Dilution Fingerprint

Ka-from-data calculator · live dilution
1. Telling strong from weak (14.6)

Equimolar solutions differ dramatically: 0.1 M HCl → pH 1.0 (100% dissociated); 0.1 M CH₃COOH → pH 2.87 (~1.3% dissociated).

Experimental discriminators: pH measurement; conductivity (weak acid conducts far less); rate with Mg/carbonate (weak acid slower at same c); and the dilution test below.

Learning goals
  • Define strength as degree of dissociation, with the equilibrium arrows: strong →, weak ⇌.
  • List three ways to distinguish experimentally at equal concentration.
Examiner tips
  • “Weak acid = dilute acid” is an instant lost mark.
2. Ka and its two assumptions (14.8–14.9)

$K_a = \dfrac{[H^+][A^-]}{[HA]}$ — the larger Ka (smaller pKa), the stronger the weak acid.

Assumption 1: [H⁺] = [A⁻] — dissociation produces them 1:1 and water’s contribution is negligible. Assumption 2: [HA]eq ≈ initial c — dissociation is under ~5%.

Together they give $[H^+] = \sqrt{K_a c}$ — the syllabus explicitly says no quadratics required.

Learning goals
  • State both assumptions when deriving — they carry marks.
  • Convert Ka ⇌ pKa fluently.
Examiner tips
  • Ka units: mol dm⁻³ (one concentration unit after cancellation).
3. Ka from data — the CP11 route (14.14, 14.23)
1
mol = mass ÷ Mr; c = mol × 1000 ÷ V(cm³).
2
Measure pH → [H⁺] = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.
3
$K_a = [H^+]^2 / c$ (assumptions stated).

Alternative CP11 method: titrate half the acid to neutralisation, mix with the other half → [HA] = [A⁻] → pH = pKa directly.

Examiner tips
  • Keep full calculator precision until the final line, then round Ka to 2–3 s.f.
4. The dilution fingerprint (14.13ii)

Dilute ×10: strong acid [H⁺] ÷10 → pH +1.00. Weak acid [H⁺] = √(Ka·c) → ÷√10 → pH +0.50.

×100 and ×1000 follow: strong +2/+3; weak +1/+1.5. Plot pH against dilution and the two gradients (1 vs 0.5 per decade) identify the acid — see it live in Module 2.

Learning goals
  • State the +1 vs +0.5 rule with its √-dependence reason.
Examiner tips
  • Questions give pH before and after dilution and ask strong-or-weak: compute the jump, then quote the rule.

Engine C · Syllabus 14.17–14.22 · the deep dive

Buffer Solutions — every point the board examines

6 cards · live buffer builder · blood preset
1. Definition & composition (14.17)
A buffer resists changes in pH when small amounts of acid or alkali are added (and on dilution).

Acidic buffer: weak acid + salt of its conjugate base — CH₃COOH + CH₃COONa (pH < 7). Basic buffer: weak base + its salt — NH₃ + NH₄Cl (pH > 7).

The essential feature: both members of a conjugate pair present in appreciable amounts — a reservoir of HA to eat added OH⁻ and of A⁻ to eat added H⁺.

Learning goals
  • Recite the definition with “resists”, not “prevents” or “keeps constant”.
  • Name one acidic and one basic buffer system.
Examiner tips
  • HCl + NaCl is NOT a buffer — the “acid” member must be WEAK. A favourite true/false stem.
2. The mechanism — two equations, always (14.18)

For CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻:

Added acid: CH₃COO⁻ + H⁺ → CH₃COOH  (conjugate base removes the H⁺)
Added alkali: CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O  (weak acid removes the OH⁻)

Why pH barely moves: each reaction converts one buffer member into the other, so the ratio [CH₃COO⁻]/[CH₃COOH] shifts only slightly — and pH = pKa + log(ratio) depends only on that ratio.

Learning goals
  • Write both equations for any given buffer pair — they are the marks.
  • Finish the explanation with the ratio argument.
Examiner tips
  • Full marks need: both equations + “ratio changes only slightly” + “pH remains almost constant”. Stress-test this live in Module 2.
3. Buffer pH calculations (14.19)
1
$[H^+] = K_a \times \dfrac{[HA]}{[A^-]}$ — or go straight to pH = pKa + log([A⁻]/[HA]).
2
Concentrations or moles — the common volume cancels in the ratio.
3
After adding strong acid/alkali: adjust the MOLES first (A⁻ ∓ x, HA ± x), then recompute.
Worked: 0.50 mol CH₃COOH + 0.20 mol CH₃COONa in 1 dm³, Ka = 1.8 × 10⁻⁵ → [H⁺] = 1.8×10⁻⁵ × 0.50/0.20 = 4.5×10⁻⁵ → pH = 4.35.
⚡ Shortcut: pH = pKa + log(mol salt / mol acid) — skip the volume entirely.
4. Preparing a buffer of a given pH (14.20)
1
From the target pH: ratio = [A⁻]/[HA] = 10^(pH − pKa).
2
Choose amounts matching that ratio (fix one, solve the other).
3
Convert to mass (mass = mol × Mr) or volume, and make up to volume.

Two practical routes: (a) dissolve weak acid + its salt directly; (b) partial neutralisation — add less-than-equivalent NaOH to the weak acid: the reaction makes A⁻ in situ and leaves HA over (e.g. 0.10 mol CH₃COOH + 0.06 mol NaOH → 0.06 mol A⁻ + 0.04 mol HA).

Examiner tips
  • Jan 2024 Q17biii: “calculate the mass of salt” — ratio first, mass last. Show the ratio or lose the method mark.
5. Buffers live on the titration curve (14.21)

In a weak-acid/strong-base titration, the flat region before the equivalence point is the buffer region — the mixture is HA + A⁻, and added OH⁻ barely moves the pH.

At half-neutralisation: exactly half the HA has become A⁻, so [HA] = [A⁻], log 1 = 0, and pH = pKa. Read the pH at V = ½Veq straight off the graph → Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴴ.

Learning goals
  • Point to the buffer region on any weak-acid curve and explain the flatness.
  • Extract Ka from the half-equivalence pH — a recurring 2-marker (Jan 2024 Q19ci).
Examiner tips
  • Strong-acid/strong-base curves have NO buffer region — don’t invent one.
6. Buffers in blood and food (14.22)

Blood (pH 7.40): the carbonic/hydrogencarbonate pair, H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻, with [HCO₃⁻]/[H₂CO₃] ≈ 20:1.

Acid enters blood: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂ + H₂O — the CO₂ is breathed out.
Alkali enters blood: H₂CO₃ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O.

Enzymes are proteins that denature outside a narrow pH range — unbuffered blood would kill. Foods: buffers resist the pH changes produced by bacterial and fungal metabolism, slowing deterioration.

Learning goals
  • Write both blood equations and quote pH 7.4.
  • Give the food-buffer reason in one sentence.
Examiner tips
  • Hit the Buffer Builder’s 🩸 blood preset — it reproduces pH 7.40 from the 20:1 ratio.

2. Why the equivalence pH is not always 7

At equivalence only the salt is present. Strong/strong gives a neutral salt (NaCl) → pH 7.

Weak acid + strong base: the salt’s anion is a weak base — CH₃COO⁻ + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COOH + OH⁻ → solution alkaline (≈ 8.7).

Strong acid + weak base: the salt’s cation is a weak acid — NH₄⁺ ⇌ NH₃ + H⁺ → solution acidic (≈ 5.3).

Learning goals
  • Explain each equivalence pH with the salt-hydrolysis equation.
Examiner tips
  • “pH 7 at equivalence” is only true for strong/strong — writing it elsewhere costs the mark.
3. Choosing the indicator (14.16)
Rule: the indicator’s colour-change range must fall within the vertical pH jump.

Phenolphthalein 8.3–10.0 (colourless ⇄ pink): weak acid/strong base ✓ and strong/strong ✓. Methyl orange 3.1–4.4 (red ⇄ yellow): strong acid/weak base ✓ and strong/strong ✓. Weak/weak: neither — no jump exists, so use a pH meter.

Learning goals
  • Quote both ranges from memory and pair each with its titration type.
  • Always add the reason: “range lies within the steep section of the curve”.
Examiner tips
  • Wrong indicator = endpoint reached too early/late → systematic error. Explain the direction when asked.
4. Diprotic acids & the half-equivalence trick (14.21)

Diprotic weak acids (e.g. ethanedioic, H₂C₂O₄) lose protons in two steps → two equivalence points and two buffer regions on the curve.

H₂SO₄ is the exception: treated as fully dissociated → one jump like a strong monoprotic acid (but [H⁺] = 2c).

Half-equivalence: at ½Veq, [HA] = [A⁻] → pH = pKa. Read it off the curve, then Ka = 10⁻ᵖᴴ — the graph method for CP11.

Learning goals
  • Explain two jumps for a weak diprotic acid; one for H₂SO₄.
  • Determine Ka from the half-equivalence pH on any weak-acid curve.
Examiner tips
  • Label the flat buffer region AND mark ½Veq when asked to show buffer action on the curve (14.21i).

▸ ⚡ Brønsted–Lowry & conjugate pairs (14.1–14.2)

Acid = proton donor · base = proton acceptor · reactions are proton transfers. Conjugate pairs differ by one H⁺.

CH₃COOH + H₂O ⇌ CH₃COO⁻ + H₃O⁺ — pairs: CH₃COOH/CH₃COO⁻ and H₃O⁺/H₂O. Water is amphoteric.

Exam tip: “proton” — never “hydrogen atom”. Pairs must differ by exactly one H⁺ with opposite charge.

▸ ⚖️ Ka, Kw and the assumptions (14.8–14.12)

$K_a = [H^+][A^-]/[HA]$ · $pK_a = -\log K_a$ · $K_w = [H^+][OH^-] = 10^{-14}$ (298 K) · $pK_w = 14$.

Weak-acid assumptions: [H⁺] = [A⁻]; [HA]eq ≈ c. Hence [H⁺] = √(Ka·c) — no quadratics.

Temperature: Kw rises with T (endothermic dissociation) → pure water pH falls below 7 when hot but stays neutral ([H⁺] = [OH⁻]).

▸ 🛡️ Buffer master recap — every board point (14.17–14.21)
  • Definition: resists pH change on adding small amounts of acid/alkali (and dilution).
  • Composition: weak acid + its salt (CH₃COOH/CH₃COONa) or weak base + its salt (NH₃/NH₄Cl).
  • Mechanism: A⁻ + H⁺ → HA (added acid) · HA + OH⁻ → A⁻ + H₂O (added alkali) · ratio changes slightly → pH almost constant.
  • pH: [H⁺] = Ka[HA]/[A⁻] or pH = pKa + log([salt]/[acid]); moles may replace concentrations.
  • Preparation: mix acid + salt, or partially neutralise the weak acid with strong base; target pH → ratio = 10^(pH−pKa) → masses.
  • On curves: flat pre-equivalence region = buffer action; at ½Veq, pH = pKa.
  • Capacity: best when [HA] ≈ [A⁻] and both concentrated; exhausted once one member is used up.
▸ 🩸 Buffers in blood & food (14.22)

Blood: H₂CO₃/HCO₃⁻ at pH 7.40, ratio ≈ 20:1. Acid: HCO₃⁻ + H⁺ → H₂CO₃ → CO₂↑ + H₂O (exhaled). Alkali: H₂CO₃ + OH⁻ → HCO₃⁻ + H₂O. Enzymes denature outside the narrow range.

Food: buffers resist pH changes from bacterial/fungal activity → slower deterioration.

▸ 🧪 CP11 & volumetric technique (14.23)

CP11 — Ka of a weak acid: measure pH of a solution of known concentration → [H⁺] → Ka = [H⁺]²/c; or half-neutralise → pH = pKa.

  • Rinse burette/pipette with their solution; flask with water only.
  • Meniscus bottom at eye level, ±0.05 cm³; white tile; swirl constantly.
  • Rough then accurate runs; concordant titres within 0.10 cm³; average.
