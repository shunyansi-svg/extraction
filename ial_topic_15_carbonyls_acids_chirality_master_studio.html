Topic 15 Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Spec 15.1 – 15.16)

Essential learning goals and examiner watch-outs across Chirality (15A), Carbonyls (15B), Carboxylic acids (15C) and their derivatives (15D).

Spec 15.1 – 15.2 Chirality & enantiomers

1. Chiral Centres & Optical Isomers

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • A chiral centre is a carbon bonded to four different atoms/groups.
  • Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images of each other.
  • Draw 3D diagrams using wedges (out of plane) and dashes (into plane).
  • Recognise that a $sp^2$ (double-bonded) carbon can never be a chiral centre.
  • Identify chiral centres in complex molecules (e.g. in drugs).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

You must state non-superimposable — "mirror images" alone is not enough (meso compounds are mirror images that ARE superimposable). Check all four groups are different.

Spec 15.3 Optical activity

2. Optical Activity & Plane-Polarised Light

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Optical activity = the ability of a single enantiomer to rotate the plane of plane-polarised monochromatic light.
  • One enantiomer rotates clockwise (dextrorotatory, +); the other rotates anticlockwise (laevorotatory, −) by the same magnitude.
  • Measured with a polarimeter.
  • The two enantiomers have identical physical properties except for the direction of rotation.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Say "plane of plane-polarised light" — ordinary light vibrates in all planes. The (+)/(−) sign cannot be predicted from structure; it must be measured.

Spec 15.4 Racemic mixture

3. Racemic Mixtures

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • A racemic mixture is a 50:50 (equimolar) mixture of two enantiomers.
  • It is optically inactive — the + and − rotations cancel exactly.
  • Formed whenever a reaction creates a chiral centre through a planar intermediate attacked from both sides with equal probability.
  • Typical examples: SN1 reactions and nucleophilic addition to carbonyls.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"Optically inactive because the rotations cancel" is the full-mark phrase. Don't say "the molecules are not chiral" — each molecule IS chiral; the mixture is inactive.

Spec 15.5 Optical evidence for mechanisms

4. Optical Activity as Mechanistic Evidence

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • SN1: planar carbocation attacked from both sides → racemic product (optically inactive).
  • SN2: back-side attack inverts the centre → single inverted enantiomer (still optically active).
  • Addition to a carbonyl (e.g. HCN): planar C=O attacked from both sides → racemic hydroxynitrile.
  • Use observed optical activity of reactants/products to distinguish mechanisms.
  • Connect "loss of optical activity" to formation of a planar intermediate.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

This links Topic 15 to Topic 11 (SN1/SN2). Always state that attack from both sides is equally likely → equal amounts of both enantiomers.

Spec 15.6 – 15.7 Carbonyl nomenclature & properties

5. Aldehydes & Ketones — Names & Physical Properties

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Aldehydes: $-\text{CHO}$, suffix -al (carbon 1 by definition). Ketones: $-\text{CO}-$, suffix -one (number the position).
  • Draw structural, displayed and skeletal formulae.
  • Carbonyls have no O–H/N–H, so they do not H-bond to themselves → lower boiling points than alcohols/acids of similar mass.
  • They can H-bond with water (C=O accepts H-bonds) → small aldehydes/ketones are water-soluble.
  • The C=O bond is polar ($\delta^+$C, $\delta^-$O) — this drives nucleophilic addition.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Distinguish "cannot H-bond to itself" from "can H-bond to water". Examiners ask why propanone boils lower than propan-1-ol but still mixes with water.

Spec 15.8 i – ii Oxidation & reduction

6. Oxidation & Reduction of Carbonyls

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • Tollens' reagent (ammoniacal $\text{AgNO}_3$): aldehydes give a silver mirror; ketones do not.
  • Fehling's / Benedict's (alkaline $\text{Cu}^{2+}$): aldehydes give a brick-red $\text{Cu}_2\text{O}$ ppt; ketones do not.
  • Acidified dichromate(VI): aldehydes → carboxylic acids (orange → green); ketones resist.
  • These tests distinguish aldehydes from ketones (aldehydes are easily oxidised).
  • LiAlH₄ in dry ether reduces aldehydes → 1° alcohols, ketones → 2° alcohols (written as $[\text{H}]$).
  • LiAlH₄ must be in dry ether — it reacts violently with water.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Know the observations exactly: silver mirror (Tollens), brick-red ppt (Fehling's), orange→green (dichromate). Ketones are negative in all three.

Spec 15.8 iii HCN addition

7. Nucleophilic Addition of HCN

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • HCN (with KCN catalyst) adds across C=O → a hydroxynitrile.
  • Mechanism: $\text{CN}^-$ attacks the $\delta^+$ carbon (nucleophilic addition); then the intermediate takes $\text{H}^+$ from HCN.
  • Draw curly arrows from the $\text{CN}^-$ lone pair to C, and from the C=O $\pi$ bond to O.
  • The planar C=O is attacked from both sides → racemic hydroxynitrile (optically inactive).
  • This reaction extends the carbon chain by one carbon.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Show the lone pair on $\text{CN}^-$, the dipoles ($\delta^+$C, $\delta^-$O), and the negative charge on the intermediate. The racemic outcome is a standard 2-mark explanation.

Spec 15.8 iv – v 2,4-DNPH & iodoform

8. 2,4-DNPH & Iodoform Tests

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • 2,4-DNPH: gives an orange/yellow precipitate with both aldehydes and ketones — a test for the carbonyl group.
  • The derivative's melting point identifies the specific carbonyl compound (compare to a data table).
  • Iodoform test ($\text{I}_2$ + alkali): positive for compounds with a $\text{CH}_3\text{C=O}$ (methyl ketone) group, or $\text{CH}_3\text{CH(OH)}$– (oxidised in situ).
  • Positive result: pale-yellow precipitate of $\text{CHI}_3$ (triiodomethane) with an antiseptic smell.
  • Ethanal is the only aldehyde that gives a positive iodoform test.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

2,4-DNPH detects any carbonyl; the iodoform test detects only the methyl carbonyl unit. Don't confuse the two — and name the yellow solid as triiodomethane.

Spec 15.9 – 15.10 Carboxylic acid names & properties

9. Carboxylic Acids — Names & Physical Properties

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Suffix -oic acid; the $-\text{COOH}$ carbon is carbon 1. Draw structural, displayed and skeletal forms.
  • Carboxylic acids form dimers via two hydrogen bonds → high boiling points.
  • They H-bond strongly with water → small acids are very soluble.
  • They are weak acids (partially dissociate): $\text{RCOOH} \rightleftharpoons \text{RCOO}^- + \text{H}^+$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The dimer (two H-bonds between two molecules) is a classic diagram question. Boiling points are higher than alcohols of similar mass.

Spec 15.11 – 15.12 Preparation & reactions

10. Making & Reacting Carboxylic Acids

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • Prepare by oxidation: 1° alcohol or aldehyde + acidified $\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$ (reflux).
  • Prepare by hydrolysis of nitriles: $\text{RCN} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{RCOOH} + \text{NH}_4^+$.
  • LiAlH₄ (dry ether) reduces acids → 1° alcohols.
  • React with bases → carboxylate salts (e.g. $\text{RCOONa}$).
  • React with PCl₅ → acyl chloride ($\text{RCOCl}$) + $\text{POCl}_3$ + $\text{HCl}$ (steamy fumes).
  • Esterification: acid + alcohol $\rightleftharpoons$ ester + water (conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ catalyst, heat).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Esterification is reversible — state the conc. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ catalyst and heat. PCl₅ gives steamy $\text{HCl}$ fumes (a test for $-\text{COOH}$).

Spec 15.13 – 15.14 Acyl chlorides & esters

11. Acyl Chlorides & Their Reactions

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • Acyl chlorides: suffix -oyl chloride ($\text{RCOCl}$); esters: alkyl alkanoate.
  • Acyl chlorides are very reactive (vigorous, no catalyst needed) — nucleophilic addition–elimination.
  • + water → carboxylic acid + $\text{HCl}$ fumes (violent).
  • + alcohol → ester + $\text{HCl}$ (goes to completion, unlike esterification).
  • + conc. ammonia → amide + $\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$ (white smoke).
  • + amines → substituted amide + $\text{HCl}$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Compare making an ester from an acyl chloride (fast, complete, no catalyst) vs. from a carboxylic acid (slow, reversible, acid catalyst). Examiners love this comparison.

Spec 15.15 – 15.16 Ester hydrolysis & polyesters

12. Ester Hydrolysis & Polyesters

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Acid hydrolysis: ester + water $\rightleftharpoons$ carboxylic acid + alcohol (dil. $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$, heat) — reversible.
  • Alkaline hydrolysis (saponification): ester + $\text{NaOH}$ → carboxylate salt + alcohol — irreversible.
  • Alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion because the salt cannot re-esterify.
  • Polyesters form by condensation polymerisation of a diol + a dicarboxylic acid (eliminating water).
  • Terylene (PET) is made from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid + ethane-1,2-diol.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Acid hydrolysis gives the acid; alkaline hydrolysis gives the salt (write $\text{RCOO}^-\text{Na}^+$, not $\text{RCOOH}$). State that condensation eliminates a small molecule.

Chirality, Optical Activity & Racemic Mixtures (Spec 15.1 – 15.5)

6 concepts mapped
1. The chiral centre
$\text{chiral centre} = \text{C bonded to 4 different groups}$

A molecule with one chiral (asymmetric) carbon exists as two optical isomers. Check that all four substituents differ. A carbon in a double bond ($sp^2$) has only three groups and can never be chiral.

Learning goals
  • Spot chiral centres, including in rings and complex molecules.
  • Rule out $sp^2$ carbons.
Examiner tips
  • Mark the chiral carbon with an asterisk (*) in exam drawings.
2. Enantiomers — non-superimposable mirror images
$\text{enantiomers} = \text{mirror images that cannot be superimposed}$

Like left and right hands. Draw one enantiomer with wedges/dashes, then draw its mirror image. They have identical boiling points, solubility, etc. — only their effect on plane-polarised light differs. See the 3D Enantiomer Lab to rotate them yourself.

Learning goals
  • Draw both enantiomers in 3D.
  • State the full definition including "non-superimposable".
Examiner tips
  • "Mirror images" alone loses a mark — always add "non-superimposable".
3. Optical activity & the polarimeter
$\text{rotation of plane-polarised monochromatic light}$

A single enantiomer rotates the plane of plane-polarised light. Dextrorotatory (+) rotates clockwise; laevorotatory (−) rotates anticlockwise. Equal magnitudes, opposite signs. Measured in a polarimeter. Try the polarimeter simulation in the 3D lab.

Learning goals
  • Define optical activity precisely.
  • Relate (+)/(−) to direction of rotation.
Examiner tips
  • The sign cannot be predicted from the structure — it is experimental.
4. Racemic mixture
$\text{racemic} = 50:50 \text{ enantiomers} \Rightarrow \text{net rotation } = 0$

An equimolar mixture of both enantiomers. The + rotation of one exactly cancels the − rotation of the other, so the mixture is optically inactive. Formed whenever a chiral centre is created via a planar intermediate attacked from both sides.

Learning goals
  • Define a racemic mixture.
  • Explain the cancellation of rotation.
Examiner tips
  • Each molecule is still chiral — the mixture is inactive, not the molecules.
5. Optical evidence for SN1 vs SN2
$\text{SN1} \rightarrow \text{racemic (inactive)}\qquad \text{SN2} \rightarrow \text{inverted (active)}$

SN1 goes through a planar carbocation attacked from both faces → racemic product (optical activity lost). SN2 is a single back-side attack → inversion of configuration, giving one enantiomer (optical activity retained, sign may flip).

Learning goals
  • Predict the optical outcome of SN1 and SN2.
  • Link planar intermediate to racemisation.
Examiner tips
  • State "attack from both sides equally likely" for the racemic mark.
6. Addition to a carbonyl → racemic
$\text{planar C=O} \xrightarrow{\ \text{CN}^-\ } \text{racemic hydroxynitrile}$

The C=O carbon is $sp^2$ and planar. A nucleophile (e.g. $\text{CN}^-$ from HCN) can attack from above or below with equal probability → a 50:50 mixture of two enantiomers → optically inactive product.

Learning goals
  • Explain racemisation in carbonyl addition.
  • Draw the planar carbonyl and both attack directions.
Examiner tips
  • This is the same reasoning as SN1 — a planar species attacked from both faces.

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Chirality
Trap 1: "Mirror images" is not enough

Enantiomers are non-superimposable mirror images. Meso compounds are mirror images that ARE superimposable — so always include both words.

Trap 2: Racemic ≠ achiral molecules

In a racemic mixture every molecule is chiral; the mixture is optically inactive because the rotations cancel. Don't say the molecules lose chirality.

Trap 3: $sp^2$ carbons

A carbon in C=O or C=C has only three groups and a planar geometry — it can never be a chiral centre. Check the hybridisation first.

Trap 4: Predicting the sign

You cannot tell from a structure whether it is (+) or (−). The direction of rotation is measured, not deduced. R/S configuration does not map to +/−.

Aldehydes & Ketones — Reactions & Tests (Spec 15.6 – 15.8)

7 concepts mapped
1. Nomenclature & formulae
$\text{aldehyde: } \text{R–CHO}\ (-\text{al})\qquad \text{ketone: } \text{R–CO–R'}\ (-\text{one})$

Aldehydes have the carbonyl at the chain end (carbon 1, e.g. propanal). Ketones have it inside the chain (number it, e.g. propanone). Draw structural, displayed and skeletal forms confidently.

Learning goals
  • Name and draw C1–C4 aldehydes and ketones.
  • Place the carbonyl correctly in each type.
Examiner tips
  • Write –CHO, never –COH. The H belongs on the carbon.
2. Physical properties — the H-bond story
$\text{no O–H} \Rightarrow \text{no self H-bonding}\ ;\ \text{C=O}\cdots\text{H–O} \Rightarrow \text{H-bond with water}$

Carbonyls cannot hydrogen-bond to themselves (no O–H), so they boil lower than alcohols/acids of similar mass (dipole–dipole only). But the C=O accepts H-bonds from water, so small aldehydes/ketones are water-soluble.

Learning goals
  • Explain boiling point vs. solubility separately.
  • Draw the C=O⋯H–O H-bond to water.
Examiner tips
  • "Soluble because it H-bonds to water" and "low b.p. because it can't H-bond to itself" are two distinct marks.
3. Oxidation — distinguishing aldehydes from ketones
$\text{RCHO} + [\text{O}] \rightarrow \text{RCOOH}\qquad \text{ketones: no reaction}$

Tollens' (ammoniacal AgNO₃): aldehyde → silver mirror. Fehling's/Benedict's (alkaline Cu²⁺): aldehyde → brick-red Cu₂O. Acidified dichromate: aldehyde → orange→green. Ketones are negative in all three.

Learning goals
  • Recall all three reagents and their positive observations.
  • Use them to distinguish an aldehyde from a ketone.
Examiner tips
  • Observations must be exact: "silver mirror", "brick-red precipitate", "orange to green".
4. Reduction with LiAlH₄
$\text{RCHO} \xrightarrow{[\text{H}]} \text{RCH}_2\text{OH}\ (1^\circ)\qquad \text{RCOR'} \xrightarrow{[\text{H}]} \text{RCH(OH)R'}\ (2^\circ)$

Lithium aluminium hydride (LiAlH₄) in dry ether reduces aldehydes to primary alcohols and ketones to secondary alcohols. Written as $[\text{H}]$. It also reduces carboxylic acids (see Tab 5).

Learning goals
  • Predict the alcohol class from the carbonyl type.
  • State the solvent: dry ether (ethoxyethane).
Examiner tips
  • LiAlH₄ reacts violently with water — "dry ether" is a required condition mark. (NaBH₄ in water/methanol is the milder alternative.)
5. HCN — nucleophilic addition
$\text{RCHO} + \text{HCN} \xrightarrow{\text{KCN}} \text{RCH(OH)CN}$ (hydroxynitrile)

The nucleophile $\text{CN}^-$ attacks the $\delta^+$ carbonyl carbon; the C=O $\pi$ electrons go to O, giving an alkoxide intermediate that picks up $\text{H}^+$ from HCN. The planar C=O is attacked from both sides → racemic product. Adds one carbon to the chain.

Learning goals
  • Draw the full curly-arrow mechanism.
  • Explain the racemic outcome.
Examiner tips
  • Show the CN lone pair, the C=O dipole, and the negative charge on O in the intermediate. See the Mechanisms tab.
6. 2,4-DNPH — the carbonyl test
$\text{aldehyde OR ketone} + \text{2,4-DNPH} \rightarrow \text{orange/yellow precipitate}$

2,4-dinitrophenylhydrazine reacts with both aldehydes and ketones to give an orange/yellow crystalline precipitate (a hydrazone). It confirms a carbonyl group is present. The derivative's melting point identifies which carbonyl compound.

Learning goals
  • State the observation and what it proves.
  • Explain identification via melting point.
Examiner tips
  • 2,4-DNPH does NOT distinguish aldehyde from ketone — it only detects C=O. Combine with Tollens' to identify fully.
7. Iodoform test — the methyl carbonyl test
$\text{CH}_3\text{C=O}\ (\text{or CH}_3\text{CH(OH)}) + \text{I}_2/\text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{CHI}_3\downarrow\ \text{(pale yellow)}$

Iodine in alkali gives a pale-yellow precipitate of triiodomethane (iodoform, CHI₃) with methyl ketones (CH₃CO–) and with CH₃CH(OH)– groups (oxidised to the methyl ketone in situ). Ethanal is the only aldehyde that gives a positive result.

Learning goals
  • Identify the structural unit that gives a positive test.
  • Name the yellow solid (triiodomethane).
Examiner tips
  • Propanone gives a positive iodoform test but ethanal is the only positive aldehyde. Ethanol also gives a positive (oxidised to ethanal).

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Carbonyls
Trap 1: 2,4-DNPH vs Tollens'

2,4-DNPH detects any carbonyl. Tollens' distinguishes aldehyde (positive) from ketone (negative). Use both together to identify an unknown.

Trap 2: LiAlH₄ vs NaBH₄ conditions

LiAlH₄ needs dry ether (reacts violently with water). NaBH₄ works in water/methanol. Both reduce C=O but LiAlH₄ also reduces –COOH.

Trap 3: Which aldehyde gives iodoform?

Only ethanal (it has the CH₃C=O unit). All other aldehydes are negative. Propanone and other methyl ketones are positive.

Trap 4: Ketones and oxidation

Ketones resist mild oxidation (no C–H on the carbonyl carbon). Only strong oxidation with C–C cleavage works — not in this spec. So ketones are negative with Tollens'/Fehling's/dichromate.

Engine 3 · Learning Suite

Carboxylic Acids, Acyl Chlorides, Esters & Polyesters (Spec 15.9 – 15.16)

7 concepts mapped
1. Carboxylic acids — names & the dimer
$\text{R–COOH}\ (-\text{oic acid});\ \text{dimer} = 2\times \text{O–H}\cdots\text{O=C H-bonds}$

The –COOH carbon is carbon 1. Two acid molecules link through two hydrogen bonds into a dimer, roughly doubling the effective mass → high boiling points. Strong H-bonding to water makes small acids very soluble. They are weak acids.

Learning goals
  • Name C1–C4 acids; draw the dimer with both H-bonds.
  • Explain b.p. and solubility in terms of H-bonding.
Examiner tips
  • The dimer diagram (two molecules, two H-bonds, shown with dotted lines) is a classic exam question.
2. Preparing carboxylic acids
$\text{RCH}_2\text{OH}/\text{RCHO} \xrightarrow{[\text{O}]\ \text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7/\text{H}^+,\ \text{reflux}} \text{RCOOH}\qquad \text{RCN} \xrightarrow{\text{H}_2\text{O}/\text{H}^+} \text{RCOOH}$

Oxidise a primary alcohol or aldehyde with acidified dichromate under reflux. Alternatively hydrolyse a nitrile (from R–X + KCN, which adds one carbon). Both are standard routes.

Learning goals
  • Give both preparation routes with conditions.
  • Note that reflux (not distillation) is needed for full oxidation.
Examiner tips
  • The nitrile route extends the chain by one carbon — a key synthesis trick.
3. Reactions of the –COOH group
$\text{LiAlH}_4\!\rightarrow\!1^\circ\text{ alcohol}\ ;\ \text{base}\!\rightarrow\!\text{salt}\ ;\ \text{PCl}_5\!\rightarrow\!\text{RCOCl}\ ;\ \text{ROH/H}^+\!\rightarrow\!\text{ester}$

LiAlH₄ (dry ether) reduces –COOH to a 1° alcohol. Bases (NaOH, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃) give carboxylate salts (effervescence with carbonates — a test for –COOH). PCl₅ gives the acyl chloride + steamy HCl. With an alcohol + conc. H₂SO₄ (heat) → ester + water (reversible).

Learning goals
  • Write all four reactions with products.
  • Use NaHCO₃ effervescence (CO₂) as the –COOH test.
Examiner tips
  • PCl₅ gives steamy HCl fumes — that observation identifies –COOH (and –OH).
4. Naming acyl chlorides & esters
$\text{RCOCl} = -\text{oyl chloride}\qquad \text{RCOOR'} = \text{alkyl alkanoate}$

Ethanoyl chloride (CH₃COCl). Esters are named in two parts: the alkyl group from the alcohol + the alkanoate from the acid — e.g. ethyl ethanoate (from ethanol + ethanoic acid).

Learning goals
  • Name acyl chlorides and esters from their structures.
  • Work backwards: name the acid and alcohol that make an ester.
Examiner tips
  • Ester = "alkyl alkanoate" — the alkyl comes from the alcohol. Getting the two parts round is a common slip.
5. Acyl chloride reactions (vigorous, no catalyst)
$\text{RCOCl} + \text{H}_2\text{O}\!\rightarrow\!\text{RCOOH}+\text{HCl}\ ;\ +\text{ROH}\!\rightarrow\!\text{RCOOR}'+\text{HCl}\ ;\ +\text{NH}_3\!\rightarrow\!\text{RCONH}_2+\text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$

Acyl chlorides are far more reactive than the parent acid. With water → acid + steamy HCl. With an alcohol → ester + HCl (goes to completion, no catalyst, no reversibility). With conc. ammonia → amide + white NH₄Cl smoke. With amines → substituted amide. All are nucleophilic addition–elimination.

Learning goals
  • Write products with water, alcohol, ammonia and amines.
  • Contrast with the reversible acid-catalysed esterification.
Examiner tips
  • Observations: steamy HCl fumes (with water/alcohol), white smoke of NH₄Cl (with ammonia).
6. Ester hydrolysis — acid vs alkaline
$\text{acid: RCOOR}'+\text{H}_2\text{O}\rightleftharpoons\text{RCOOH}+\text{R}'\text{OH}\ ;\ \text{alkali: }\rightarrow\text{RCOO}^-\text{Na}^++\text{R}'\text{OH}$

Acid hydrolysis (dil. H₂SO₄, heat) is the reversible reverse of esterification, giving the acid + alcohol. Alkaline hydrolysis (NaOH, heat) — saponification — is irreversible, giving the carboxylate salt + alcohol. Soap-making uses this.

Learning goals
  • Give the products of each hydrolysis type.
  • Explain why alkaline hydrolysis goes to completion.
Examiner tips
  • Alkaline hydrolysis gives the salt (RCOO⁻Na⁺), not the acid — a frequent lost mark.
7. Polyesters — condensation polymerisation
$\text{diol} + \text{dicarboxylic acid} \rightarrow \text{polyester} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

A diol and a dicarboxylic acid join repeatedly, eliminating water each time (condensation polymerisation). Terylene (PET) is made from benzene-1,4-dicarboxylic acid (terephthalic acid) + ethane-1,2-diol. The repeat unit contains the ester link –COO–.

Learning goals
  • Define condensation polymerisation.
  • Draw the repeat unit of Terylene.
Examiner tips
  • State that a small molecule (water) is eliminated. Show the ester link clearly in the repeat unit.

6. LiAlH₄ + carboxylic acid

Product: primary alcohol

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Acids & Derivatives
Trap 1: Two ways to make an ester

Acid + alcohol is reversible, slow, acid-catalysed. Acyl chloride + alcohol is fast, complete, no catalyst. Examiners ask you to compare them.

Trap 2: Hydrolysis products

Acid hydrolysis → the carboxylic acid. Alkaline hydrolysis → the carboxylate salt. Write RCOO⁻Na⁺ for the alkaline case, not RCOOH.

Trap 3: Ester naming order

"Ethyl ethanoate" — the alkyl (ethyl) comes from the alcohol, the alkanoate (ethanoate) from the acid. Don't swap them.

Trap 4: Reflux vs distillation in oxidation

To get the carboxylic acid from a 1° alcohol you must reflux (full oxidation). Distilling off the product stops you at the aldehyde.

Mechanisms — Curly Arrows & Stereochemical Outcomes (Spec 15.5, 15.8 iii)

6 mechanisms mapped
1. Nucleophilic addition of HCN to a carbonyl
$\overset{\delta^-}{\text{C}}\text{N}^- \rightarrow \delta^+\text{C}\ ;\ \pi_{\text{C=O}} \rightarrow \text{O}\ ;\ \text{then } \text{O}^- + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{OH}$

Step 1: the $\text{CN}^-$ lone pair attacks the $\delta^+$ carbonyl carbon; the C=O $\pi$ bond moves onto oxygen, giving a tetrahedral alkoxide. Step 2: the alkoxide takes $\text{H}^+$ from HCN → hydroxynitrile. Because the planar C=O is attacked from either face with equal chance, the product is racemic.

Learning goals
  • Draw both curly arrows correctly (CN→C and C=O→O).
  • Show the negative charge on O in the intermediate.
  • State the racemic outcome with reasoning.
Examiner tips
  • Mark the dipoles ($\delta^+$C, $\delta^-$O) and the CN lone pair. Arrows must start from a lone pair/bond and end on an atom/bond.
2. Nucleophilic addition–elimination (acyl chlorides)
$\text{Nu}^- \text{ adds to C=O} \rightarrow \text{tetrahedral intermediate} \rightarrow \text{Cl}^- \text{ leaves, C=O reforms}$

A nucleophile (H₂O, ROH, NH₃, RNH₂) attacks the carbonyl carbon, opening the C=O onto oxygen. The intermediate then expels chloride (an excellent leaving group), reforming C=O. Net result: the Cl is substituted by the nucleophile. No catalyst needed — the C=O is very electrophilic and Cl⁻ leaves easily.

Learning goals
  • Describe the add → eliminate sequence.
  • Explain why acyl chlorides are so reactive.
Examiner tips
  • This is "addition–elimination", not simple substitution — name the mechanism type.
3. Esterification (acid-catalysed condensation)
$\text{RCOOH} + \text{R}'\text{OH} \underset{\text{heat}}{\overset{\text{conc. H}_2\text{SO}_4}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{RCOOR}' + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

The acid catalyst protonates the carbonyl oxygen, making the carbon more electrophilic; the alcohol attacks; water is eliminated. It is reversible, so the yield is limited unless you remove water or use excess reagent.

Learning goals
  • State the catalyst and conditions.
  • Explain the reversibility and how to improve yield.
Examiner tips
  • Write the equilibrium arrows. Conc. H₂SO₄ is a catalyst AND a dehydrating agent.
4. Acid hydrolysis of an ester
$\text{RCOOR}' + \text{H}_2\text{O} \underset{\text{heat}}{\overset{\text{dil. H}^+}{\rightleftharpoons}} \text{RCOOH} + \text{R}'\text{OH}$

The exact reverse of esterification. Dilute acid and heat with excess water drive it toward the acid + alcohol. It is reversible.

Learning goals
  • Give reagents and products.
  • Relate it to the reverse of esterification.
Examiner tips
  • Products are the carboxylic ACID and the alcohol.
5. Alkaline hydrolysis (saponification)
$\text{RCOOR}' + \text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\text{heat}} \text{RCOO}^- + \text{R}'\text{OH}$

Hydroxide attacks the carbonyl carbon; the intermediate expels the alkoxide (RO⁻), which is immediately protonated by the acid formed, but under basic conditions the acid is deprotonated to the carboxylate salt. Because the carboxylate cannot be attacked by RO⁻, the reaction is irreversible and goes to completion.

Learning goals
  • Give the salt + alcohol products.
  • Explain why it is irreversible.
Examiner tips
  • Write the carboxylate ion/salt, not the acid. This is how soap is made from fats (tri-esters of glycerol).
6. Stereochemical outcomes — the summary
$\text{planar intermediate} \Rightarrow \text{racemic}\ ;\ \text{concerted back-side} \Rightarrow \text{inversion}$

Any mechanism that creates a chiral centre via a planar species (SN1 carbocation, or C=O addition) gives a racemic mixture (optically inactive). A concerted back-side attack (SN2) gives inversion of configuration (one enantiomer, still optically active). Use observed optical activity to distinguish these.

Learning goals
  • Match each mechanism to its stereochemical outcome.
  • Use optical activity data as mechanistic evidence.
Examiner tips
  • This is a favourite synoptic question linking Topic 15 to Topic 11. Always state "attack from both sides equally likely".

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Mechanisms
Trap 1: Addition vs addition–elimination

HCN on a carbonyl is addition (nothing leaves). Acyl chlorides undergo addition–elimination (Cl⁻ leaves). Name the mechanism precisely.

Trap 2: Curly arrow origins

Arrows start from a lone pair or a bond, never from an atom or a charge. The CN arrow starts on the C lone pair; the C=O arrow starts on the π bond.

Trap 3: Racemic vs inverted

Planar intermediate (SN1, C=O addition) → racemic. Concerted back-side attack (SN2) → inversion. Don't mix them up.

Trap 4: Forgetting the intermediate charge

In carbonyl addition the oxygen gains a negative charge in the intermediate. Omitting it (or the subsequent protonation) loses marks.
