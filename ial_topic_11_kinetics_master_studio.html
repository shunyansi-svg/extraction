Spec 11.1 i–v Rate & rate equation terms

1. Rate of Reaction & the Rate Equation

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define rate of reaction as the change in concentration of a reactant or product per unit time ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$).
  • Write the general rate equation $\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$ where $m$ and $n$ are $0$, $1$ or $2$.
  • Define order with respect to a substance as the power to which its concentration is raised in the rate equation.
  • Define overall order as the sum of all the powers ($m+n+\dots$).
  • State that orders are experimental — they cannot be read from the stoichiometric equation.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The most common error is deducing order from balancing numbers. For $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$ the orders are not automatically 2 and 1 — they come from data. Always write "determined experimentally".

Spec 11.1 vi–ix t½, RDS, $E_a$, catalysts

2. Half-life, Rate-Determining Step, $E_a$ & Catalysts

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Define half-life ($t_{1/2}$) as the time for the concentration of a reactant to fall to half its initial value.
  • Define the rate-determining step as the slowest step in a reaction mechanism.
  • Define activation energy as the minimum energy required for a collision to result in reaction.
  • Distinguish a homogeneous catalyst (same phase as reactants) from a heterogeneous catalyst (different phase, usually solid with gaseous reactants).
  • State that a catalyst provides an alternative pathway of lower $E_a$ and is unchanged at the end.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A catalyst lowers $E_a$ — it never "provides energy" or "increases collision frequency". Raising temperature does not lower $E_a$; it increases the fraction of molecules with $E \ge E_a$.

Spec 11.2 Half-life & first order

3. Half-life Calculations & Identifying First Order

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Read $t_{1/2}$ from a concentration–time graph: time to drop from $[A]_0 \rightarrow \tfrac{1}{2}[A]_0$, then $\tfrac{1}{2} \rightarrow \tfrac{1}{4}[A]_0$.
  • Identify a constant half-life as the signature of a first-order reaction.
  • Use $t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k} = \dfrac{0.693}{k}$ to link half-life and the rate constant for first order.
  • Recognise that for zero order successive half-lives decrease, and for second order they increase.
  • Calculate $k$ from a measured half-life (first order only).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

$t_{1/2} = 0.693/k$ applies only to first order. Measure at least two successive half-lives on the graph to prove they are constant before claiming first order.

Spec 11.3 Experimental techniques

4. Selecting & Justifying a Method to Follow Rate

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • Titration: withdraw timed samples, quench, titrate (e.g. $\text{I}_2$ with standard thiosulfate) — for reactions in solution.
  • Colorimetry: follow a coloured species ($\text{I}_2$, $\text{MnO}_4^-$, $\text{NO}_2$) — absorbance $\propto$ concentration.
  • Mass change: loss of mass as a gas escapes (e.g. $\text{CO}_2$ from marble chips + $\text{HCl}$).
  • Volume of gas: gas syringe / inverted cylinder (e.g. $\text{H}_2$ from $\text{Mg} + \text{HCl}$).
  • Other methods: pressure change (constant volume), conductivity (ions formed/removed), pH, clock timing.
  • Justify the choice by linking the measured property to a species whose concentration changes.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

You must justify, not just name. E.g. colorimetry works for $\text{I}_2$ because it is coloured, but not for a colourless system. Mass change fails for $\text{H}_2$ (too light) — use a gas syringe instead.

Spec 11.4 Initial-rate & monitoring

5. Initial-Rate Method & Continuous Monitoring

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Initial-rate method: run separate experiments varying the initial concentration of one reagent while keeping others constant.
  • Understand that a clock reaction is an acceptable approximation of the initial-rate method.
  • Continuous monitoring: record concentration (or volume/pressure) against time in a single run.
  • Use continuous data to plot concentration–time or volume–time graphs.
  • Obtain the initial rate from the tangent at $t=0$ on a concentration–time curve.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

In the initial-rate method only one concentration changes per set of runs — everything else (temperature, other reagents, total volume) must be constant. State "keep temperature constant" for full marks.

Spec 11.5 Deducing order 0/1/2

6. Deducing Order from Graphs & Initial Rates

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • Concentration–time: zero order = straight line; first order = constant $t_{1/2}$; second order = $t_{1/2}$ increases.
  • Rate–concentration: zero order = horizontal line; first order = straight line through origin; second order = upward curve (parabola).
  • Initial rates: doubling $[A]$ → rate $\times 2$ = first order; $\times 4$ = second order; unchanged = zero order.
  • Use ratios: $\dfrac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1} = \left(\dfrac{[A]_2}{[A]_1}\right)^m$ to solve for $m$.
  • Repeat for each reactant, then assemble the full rate equation.
  • Calculate $k$ by substituting one experiment's data, with correct units.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A rate–concentration graph for second order is a curve through the origin, not a straight line. When comparing experiments, pick two runs where only the target reactant changes.

Spec 11.6 Iodination of propanone

7. Acid-Catalysed Iodination of Propanone

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Know the reaction: $\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3 + \text{I}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{I} + \text{HI}$.
  • Know the experimental orders: first order in propanone, first order in $\text{H}^+$, zero order in $\text{I}_2$.
  • Rate equation: $\text{rate} = k[\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3][\text{H}^+]$ — iodine does not appear.
  • Predict that propanone and $\text{H}^+$ are involved in (or before) the rate-determining step; $\text{I}_2$ reacts after it.
  • Deduce a mechanism: slow enolisation (propanone + $\text{H}^+$), then fast reaction with $\text{I}_2$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Zero order in $\text{I}_2$ is the key deduction — changing $[\text{I}_2]$ does not change rate, so $\text{I}_2$ is not in the RDS. $\text{H}^+$ is a catalyst: it appears in the rate equation but is regenerated.

Spec 11.7 – 11.8 RDS & mechanisms

8. Rate-Determining Step ⇄ Rate Equation & Mechanisms

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • From mechanism → rate equation: only species in the RDS (and steps before it) appear.
  • From rate equation → RDS: each concentration term corresponds to a species in the slow step.
  • The RDS is the step with the highest activation energy / lowest rate.
  • Deduce a mechanism that (a) sums to the stoichiometric equation and (b) matches the rate equation.
  • Check intermediates cancel when adding the elementary steps.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A species that is zero order may still appear in the mechanism — just after the RDS. Catalysts and intermediates never appear in the overall balanced equation, but a catalyst can appear in the rate equation.

Spec 11.9 SN1 vs SN2 evidence

9. Hydrolysis of Halogenoalkanes — SN1 & SN2 Evidence

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Tertiary halogenoalkane hydrolysis: $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}]$ (first order) → SN1, two-step via a carbocation.
  • Primary halogenoalkane hydrolysis: $\text{rate} = k[\text{R–X}][\text{OH}^-]$ (second order) → SN2, one concerted step.
  • Explain that the rate equation shows which species are in the RDS, hence the mechanism.
  • SN1: slow ionisation is the RDS; nucleophile attacks the planar carbocation afterwards.
  • SN2: nucleophile attack and leaving-group departure occur together in the single RDS.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Don't write $\text{OH}^-$ in the SN1 rate equation — only the halogenoalkane is in the RDS. The kinetic evidence is what distinguishes the two mechanisms; structure (1°/3°) explains why each pathway is favoured.

Spec 11.10 Activation energy

10. Finding $E_a$ — the Arrhenius Equation

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Use the Arrhenius equation $k = A\mathrm{e}^{-E_a/RT}$ (given if needed) and its linear form $\ln k = \ln A - \dfrac{E_a}{R}\cdot\dfrac{1}{T}$.
  • Plot $\ln k$ (or $\ln$ rate) against $1/T$ — a straight line with gradient $= -E_a/R$.
  • Calculate $E_a = -\text{gradient} \times R$ with $R = 8.31\ \text{J K}^{-1}\text{ mol}^{-1}$.
  • Convert $E_a$ from $\text{J mol}^{-1}$ to $\text{kJ mol}^{-1}$ (divide by 1000).
  • Use the two-temperature form $\ln\dfrac{k_2}{k_1} = \dfrac{E_a}{R}\left(\dfrac{1}{T_1} - \dfrac{1}{T_2}\right)$ when only two data points are given.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Temperature must be in kelvin ($+273$). The gradient is negative, so $E_a$ comes out positive — don't report a negative activation energy. Forgetting the J→kJ conversion is the classic lost mark.

Spec 11.11 Heterogeneous catalysis

11. Solid (Heterogeneous) Catalysts in Industry

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Explain that a solid catalyst provides a surface on which gaseous reactants adsorb.
  • Adsorption weakens bonds in the reactants, lowering $E_a$ and increasing rate.
  • Describe the sequence: adsorption → reaction on surface → desorption of products.
  • Give industrial examples: Fe (Haber), Ni (hydrogenation), $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ (Contact), Pt/Rh (catalytic converter).
  • State that the catalyst is unchanged chemically at the end and does not alter the equilibrium position.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A catalyst speeds up the forward and reverse reactions equally — equilibrium is reached faster but the position and $K$ are unchanged. Use the word adsorption (surface), not absorption (bulk).

Spec 11.12 – 11.13 Core Practicals 9a/9b/10

12. Core Practicals — Iodine–Propanone, Iodine Clock & $E_a$

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • CP 9a: follow the iodine–propanone reaction by titration — timed samples, quench, titrate excess $\text{I}_2$ with thiosulfate.
  • CP 9b (Harcourt–Esson clock): $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 2\text{I}^- + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$ with a little thiosulfate + starch.
  • Explain why the clock works: thiosulfate mops up $\text{I}_2$ until it runs out, then starch turns blue-black.
  • Relate time to rate: $1/t \propto$ initial rate; vary one reactant to find its order.
  • CP 10: measure rate at several temperatures, plot $\ln k$ vs $1/T$, find $E_a$ from the gradient.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

In the clock reaction the thiosulfate must be small and constant so reactant concentrations barely change during the timed interval. Add starch near the end-point of a thiosulfate titration, not at the start.

Rate Equations, Order & the Initial-Rate Method (Spec 11.1, 11.5)

8 concepts mapped
1. The rate equation
$\text{rate} = k[A]^m[B]^n$

$k$ = rate constant (depends on temperature & catalyst). $m$, $n$ = orders (0, 1 or 2), found experimentally. The rate equation links rate to the concentrations of the species that control the slow step.

Learning goals
  • Identify each symbol in $\text{rate}=k[A]^m[B]^n$.
  • State that orders are experimental, not from stoichiometry.
  • Recognise that only some reactants may appear.
Examiner tips
  • Never deduce $m,n$ from balancing coefficients.
  • A reactant absent from the rate equation is zero order.
2. Order & overall order
$\text{order in }A = m \qquad \text{overall order} = m+n$

Order with respect to a substance = the power to which its concentration is raised. Overall order = sum of all powers. E.g. $\text{rate}=k[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]$ is 2nd order in NO, 1st in O₂, 3rd overall.

Learning goals
  • Read individual and overall orders from a given rate equation.
  • State orders using the words "first/second/zero order in X".
Examiner tips
  • Give both the order in each species AND the overall order when asked.
  • "Third order overall" is acceptable; individual orders are rarely >2.
3. Units of the rate constant $k$
$\text{rate} = k[A]^m \Rightarrow k = \dfrac{\text{rate}}{[A]^m}$

Derive units by rearranging. Zero order: $\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$. First order: $\text{s}^{-1}$. Second order: $\text{mol}^{-1}\text{ dm}^{3}\text{ s}^{-1}$. Third order: $\text{mol}^{-2}\text{ dm}^{6}\text{ s}^{-1}$.

Learning goals
  • Derive $k$ units for overall orders 0, 1, 2, 3.
  • Substitute rate ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}\text{ s}^{-1}$) and concentration ($\text{mol dm}^{-3}$) correctly.
Examiner tips
  • Units of $k$ change with overall order — a common 1-mark gift.
  • First order $k$ has units $\text{s}^{-1}$ (concentration cancels).
4. The initial-rate method
$\text{vary one }[X] \text{, keep all else constant} \rightarrow \text{measure initial rate}$

Run several experiments. In each set only one reactant's initial concentration changes; temperature, other concentrations and total volume stay fixed. Compare how the initial rate responds. A clock reaction gives an approximate initial rate ($1/t$).

Learning goals
  • Design an initial-rate experiment (what to vary, what to fix).
  • Explain why only one concentration may change at a time.
  • Link a clock reaction's $1/t$ to the initial rate.
Examiner tips
  • State "constant temperature" — $k$ depends on it.
  • Keep total volume constant by topping up with water/solvent.
5. Deducing order by ratio (worked)
$\dfrac{\text{rate}_2}{\text{rate}_1} = \left(\dfrac{[A]_2}{[A]_1}\right)^{m}$

Pick two runs where only $[A]$ changes. If $[A]$ doubles and rate doubles: $2 = 2^m \Rightarrow m=1$. If rate quadruples: $4 = 2^m \Rightarrow m=2$. If rate unchanged: $1 = 2^m \Rightarrow m=0$. Repeat for each reactant, then write the rate equation.

Learning goals
  • Set up the ratio equation for each reactant.
  • Solve for $m$ when the factor is 2, 3 or 4.
  • Assemble the full rate equation from individual orders.
Examiner tips
  • Show the ratio working — method marks are awarded even if the final order slips.
  • Only compare runs where the other reactant is constant.
6. Calculating the rate constant $k$
$k = \dfrac{\text{rate}}{[A]^m[B]^n}$

Once orders are known, substitute the rate and concentrations from one experiment. $k$ is constant at a given temperature, so any run gives the same value (within experimental error).

Learning goals
  • Substitute one experiment's data into the rate equation.
  • Give $k$ with the correct value and units.
Examiner tips
  • Use initial rates and initial concentrations together.
  • Missing/wrong units for $k$ is the most common lost mark here.
7. What zero order means
$\text{rate} = k[X]^0 = k$

Changing $[X]$ has no effect on rate. $X$ is not involved in the rate-determining step (it reacts afterwards). In the iodination of propanone, $\text{I}_2$ is zero order. On a rate–concentration graph, zero order is a horizontal line.

Learning goals
  • Interpret zero order as "not in the RDS".
  • Recognise a horizontal rate–concentration line as zero order.
Examiner tips
  • Zero order ≠ "not involved in the reaction" — it is involved, just after the RDS.
  • Don't omit a zero-order species from the mechanism.
8. Worked example: $2\text{NO} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{NO}_2$
$\text{rate} = k[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]$

Data: doubling $[\text{NO}]$ (O₂ fixed) quadruples rate → 2nd order in NO. Doubling $[\text{O}_2]$ (NO fixed) doubles rate → 1st order in O₂. Overall order = 3. Units of $k$: $\text{mol}^{-2}\text{ dm}^{6}\text{ s}^{-1}$. Here the orders happen to match the coefficients — but that is coincidence, not a rule.

Learning goals
  • Work through a full initial-rate table to the rate equation.
  • State overall order and $k$ units for a third-order reaction.
Examiner tips
  • Examiners love this reaction because the orders match the equation — students wrongly generalise this.
  • Always justify each order with a data comparison.

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Rate Equations
Trap 1: Stoichiometry vs experiment

Wrong: reading orders from balancing numbers.
Right: orders come only from initial-rate data. $2\text{NO}+\text{O}_2$ happens to give $[\text{NO}]^2[\text{O}_2]$, but that is a coincidence to be verified, not assumed.

Trap 2: Zero order vs not involved

Zero order: species reacts after the RDS (e.g. $\text{I}_2$ in propanone iodination).
Not involved: species is not in the mechanism at all. These are different statements.

Trap 3: $k$ constant vs rate constant

$k$ is constant only at a fixed temperature. Change $T$ or add a catalyst and $k$ changes. Concentration changes alter the rate, not $k$.

Trap 4: Order in vs overall order

"Order with respect to A" is a single power. "Overall order" is the sum. A question asking for "the order" of the reaction means the overall order.

Concentration–Time & Rate–Concentration Graphs, Half-life (Spec 11.2, 11.5)

7 concepts mapped
📈 Concentration–time profiles for the three orders

Zero order falls in a straight line; first order decays with a constant half-life; second order drops fastest at the start but tails off most slowly. The dashed line marks ½[A]₀.

1. Concentration–time: zero order
$[A] = [A]_0 - kt \quad$ (straight line, gradient $=-k$)

A plot of $[A]$ against $t$ is a straight line sloping down. The rate is constant — independent of concentration. Successive half-lives get shorter.

Learning goals
  • Recognise a linear $[A]$–t plot as zero order.
  • Read $k$ from the magnitude of the gradient.
Examiner tips
  • Zero order is rare in simple solution reactions — often a surface/enzyme-catalysed case.
2. Concentration–time: first order
$\ln[A] = \ln[A]_0 - kt \quad$ (constant $t_{1/2}$)

$[A]$–t is an exponential decay with a constant half-life: each drop $[A]_0 \to \tfrac12[A]_0 \to \tfrac14[A]_0$ takes the same time. A plot of $\ln[A]$ vs $t$ is a straight line (gradient $=-k$).

Learning goals
  • Identify first order from constant successive half-lives.
  • Know $\ln[A]$ vs $t$ is linear for first order.
Examiner tips
  • "Constant half-life ⇒ first order" is a standard one-line deduction worth a mark.
3. Concentration–time: second order
$\dfrac{1}{[A]} = \dfrac{1}{[A]_0} + kt \quad$ ($t_{1/2}$ increases)

$ [A]$–t falls steeply at first, then levels off more slowly than first order. Successive half-lives double (each is longer than the last). A plot of $1/[A]$ vs $t$ is a straight line.

Learning goals
  • Distinguish second from first order by increasing half-life.
  • Know $1/[A]$ vs $t$ is linear for second order.
Examiner tips
  • First vs second on $[A]$–t alone is subtle — always confirm with half-lives or a rate–concentration plot.
4. Rate–concentration graphs
$\text{rate vs }[A]:\; 0^\circ \to \text{horizontal};\; 1^\circ \to \text{line through origin};\; 2^\circ \to \text{parabola}$

These are the clearest order fingerprints. Zero order: rate constant (horizontal). First order: straight line through the origin ($\text{rate}\propto[A]$). Second order: upward curve through the origin ($\text{rate}\propto[A]^2$).

Learning goals
  • Match each of the three rate–$[A]$ shapes to its order.
  • Build a rate–$[A]$ graph from initial-rate data (plot rate vs $[A]$).
Examiner tips
  • Second order is a curve through the origin, not a straight line — a frequent MCQ distractor.
5. Half-life and the rate constant
$t_{1/2} = \dfrac{\ln 2}{k} = \dfrac{0.693}{k}\quad$ (first order only)

For a first-order reaction, $t_{1/2}$ is independent of $[A]_0$ and inversely proportional to $k$. Rearrange to $k = 0.693/t_{1/2}$. For zero order $t_{1/2}=[A]_0/2k$; for second order $t_{1/2}=1/(k[A]_0)$.

Learning goals
  • Calculate $k$ from a first-order half-life.
  • Recall which $t_{1/2}$ formula applies to which order.
Examiner tips
  • Only use $0.693/k$ for first order — state the order first to justify the formula.
6. Initial rate from a tangent at $t=0$
$\text{initial rate} = \left|\text{gradient of tangent at } t=0\right|$

On a concentration–time curve, draw a tangent at the origin and take its gradient (rise/run, with units). This gives the initial rate for that run — the value used in the initial-rate method.

Learning goals
  • Draw a tangent at $t=0$ and compute its gradient with units.
  • Explain why the initial rate (not an average) is used for orders.
Examiner tips
  • Use the tangent, not a chord across the curve. Show the triangle you measured.
7. Reading half-life from a graph
$[A]_0 \rightarrow \tfrac12[A]_0 \rightarrow \tfrac14[A]_0 \rightarrow \tfrac18[A]_0$

Read the time to fall to half, then to a quarter, of the starting concentration. If the two intervals are equal → first order. Compare several half-lives to be certain.

Learning goals
  • Measure at least two successive half-lives from data.
  • Use their constancy (or not) to assign the order.
Examiner tips
  • One half-life is not proof — measure two or three to show the trend.

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Graphs & Half-life
Trap 1: First vs second order decay

Both look like falling curves. First: constant $t_{1/2}$. Second: $t_{1/2}$ increases. Decide by measuring successive half-lives, not by eye.

Trap 2: Rate–conc line vs curve

First order = straight line through origin. Second order = curve through origin. Both pass through the origin — the shape is the discriminator.

Trap 3: $t_{1/2}$ formula scope

$t_{1/2}=0.693/k$ is first order only. For zero order $t_{1/2}=[A]_0/2k$; for second order $t_{1/2}=1/(k[A]_0)$ — both depend on $[A]_0$.

Trap 4: Tangent vs chord

Initial rate uses the tangent at $t=0$. A chord between two points gives an average rate over that interval — a different (smaller) number.

Engine 3 · Learning Suite

Reaction Mechanisms, the RDS & SN1/SN2 Evidence (Spec 11.6 – 11.9)

7 concepts mapped
1. What is the rate-determining step?
$\text{RDS} = \text{slowest step} = \text{highest } E_a$

Most reactions occur in several elementary steps. The slowest step limits the overall rate — like the narrowest part of a funnel. It has the highest activation energy. Only the species taking part in the RDS (and in fast steps before it) appear in the rate equation.

Learning goals
  • Define the RDS as the slowest step / highest $E_a$.
  • Explain why the overall rate cannot exceed the RDS rate.
Examiner tips
  • "Slowest step in the mechanism" is the exact phrase examiners award.
2. From mechanism to rate equation
$\text{write the RDS} \rightarrow \text{its reactants become the rate terms}$

Identify the slow step. Each reactant in it contributes a concentration term, raised to the power equal to how many molecules of it participate. Species in fast steps after the RDS do not appear.

Learning goals
  • Write the rate equation from a given two-step mechanism.
  • Identify which species are zero order from the mechanism.
Examiner tips
  • If a fast equilibrium precedes the RDS, its reactants can also appear — at IAL the RDS reactants are usually enough.
3. From rate equation to the RDS
$\text{rate}=k[A][B] \Rightarrow \text{RDS involves 1 } A \text{ and 1 } B$

Each concentration term tells you a molecule of that species is in the slow step. Propose elementary steps that (a) include those species in the RDS, (b) sum to the overall equation, and (c) cancel all intermediates.

Learning goals
  • Deduce the composition of the RDS from a rate equation.
  • Check that proposed steps sum to the stoichiometric equation.
Examiner tips
  • Label each step "slow" or "fast" — examiners look for this.
4. Acid-catalysed iodination of propanone
$\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3 + \text{I}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{I} + \text{HI}$

Experiment: first order in propanone, first order in $\text{H}^+$, zero order in $\text{I}_2$. So $\text{rate} = k[\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3][\text{H}^+]$. Iodine concentration does not affect the rate — it reacts after the RDS. $\text{H}^+$ is a catalyst (regenerated).

Learning goals
  • State the orders and the rate equation for this reaction.
  • Explain the significance of zero order in $\text{I}_2$.
Examiner tips
  • This is a favourite exam context — memorise the rate equation and the "zero order in iodine" conclusion.
5. A mechanism for propanone iodination
$\text{slow: } \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3 + \text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{intermediate (enol)}$
$\text{fast: } \text{intermediate} + \text{I}_2 \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{I} + \text{H}^+ + \text{I}^-$

The slow step involves propanone and $\text{H}^+$ (matching the rate equation). Iodine reacts in the fast step afterwards. $\text{H}^+$ is used then regenerated — a catalyst. The intermediate (an enol) cancels when the steps are added.

Learning goals
  • Match each step to the experimental rate equation.
  • Show that the steps sum to the overall equation.
Examiner tips
  • Any mechanism that puts propanone + $\text{H}^+$ in the slow step and $\text{I}_2$ afterwards is acceptable — you are proposing a possible mechanism.
6. SN1 evidence — tertiary halogenoalkane
$(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr} + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow (\text{CH}_3)_3\text{COH} + \text{Br}^-$
$\text{rate} = k[(\text{CH}_3)_3\text{CBr}]$

First order — only the halogenoalkane is in the RDS. Mechanism: slow ionisation to a planar carbocation + $\text{Br}^-$, then fast attack by $\text{OH}^-$. This two-step pathway is SN1. Favoured for 3° because the carbocation is stable.

Learning goals
  • Link a first-order rate equation to an SN1 mechanism.
  • Describe the carbocation intermediate and its planar shape.
Examiner tips
  • $\text{OH}^-$ is absent from the rate equation — do not include it in the RDS.
7. SN2 evidence — primary halogenoalkane
$\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Br} + \text{OH}^- \rightarrow \text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{OH} + \text{Br}^-$
$\text{rate} = k[\text{CH}_3\text{CH}_2\text{Br}][\text{OH}^-]$

Second order — both species in the RDS. Mechanism: a single concerted step where $\text{OH}^-$ attacks as $\text{Br}^-$ leaves (one transition state). This is SN2. Favoured for 1° because there is little steric hindrance.

Learning goals
  • Link a second-order rate equation to an SN2 mechanism.
  • Contrast the one-step SN2 with the two-step SN1.
Examiner tips
  • The kinetic evidence (order) is what distinguishes SN1 from SN2 — state it explicitly.

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Mechanisms & RDS
Trap 1: SN1 vs SN2 rate equations

SN1: rate = $k[\text{R–X}]$ (nucleophile absent). SN2: rate = $k[\text{R–X}][\text{nucleophile}]$. The presence/absence of the nucleophile in the rate equation is the diagnostic.

Trap 2: Catalyst vs intermediate

Catalyst: used then regenerated (appears in rate equation, not overall equation). Intermediate: formed then consumed (appears in neither overall equation nor rate equation).

Trap 3: Zero order ≠ absent

A zero-order species (e.g. $\text{I}_2$) still appears in the mechanism — in a fast step after the RDS. Don't delete it from the mechanism.

Trap 4: "The" mechanism

Kinetics suggests a possible mechanism, not a unique one. Write "a possible mechanism consistent with the rate equation" and make the steps sum correctly.

Experimental Methods & Core Practicals 9a / 9b / 10 (Spec 11.3, 11.4, 11.12, 11.13)

7 concepts mapped
1. Titration (quench-and-titrate)
$\text{sample at time } t \rightarrow \text{quench} \rightarrow \text{titrate remaining reactant}$

Withdraw small samples at timed intervals and quench them (cool rapidly / add to excess reagent) so the reaction stops. Titrate the remaining reactant (e.g. iodine with standard sodium thiosulfate, starch near the end-point). Plot concentration vs time.

Learning goals
  • Describe the withdraw–quench–titrate sequence.
  • Explain why quenching is essential.
Examiner tips
  • Without quenching the reaction continues during titration → wrong concentrations. State it.

Safety: Iodine stains and is an irritant; thiosulfate titrations use dilute reagents — standard eye protection.

2. Colorimetry
$\text{absorbance} \propto [\text{coloured species}]$

A colorimeter measures how much light a coloured solution absorbs. Works when a reactant or product is coloured — e.g. brown $\text{I}_2$, purple $\text{MnO}_4^-$, brown $\text{NO}_2$. Continuous, non-invasive monitoring. First calibrate with standard solutions.

Learning goals
  • State the requirement: a coloured species whose concentration changes.
  • Describe calibration with known concentrations.
Examiner tips
  • Colorimetry fails for colourless systems — justify its use by naming the coloured species.
3. Mass change & gas volume
$\text{mass loss} \leftrightarrow \text{gas evolved}\qquad \text{gas syringe} \rightarrow V(t)$

Mass change: reaction on a balance; mass falls as a heavy gas (e.g. $\text{CO}_2$) escapes — good for marble chips + $\text{HCl}$. Gas volume: collect gas in a syringe or inverted cylinder; record volume vs time — good for $\text{H}_2$ from $\text{Mg} + \text{HCl}$.

Learning goals
  • Choose mass loss for heavy gases, gas syringe for light gases.
  • Convert gas volume to concentration if needed.
Examiner tips
  • Mass change is poor for $\text{H}_2$ (mass loss too small to measure well) — use a gas syringe instead.
4. Other suitable techniques
$\text{pressure (const. }V\text{)} \;\cdot\; \text{conductivity} \;\cdot\; \text{pH} \;\cdot\; \text{clock timing}$

Pressure: for gas-phase reactions with a change in mole number, at constant volume. Conductivity: when the number of ions changes (e.g. $\text{OH}^-$ consumed). pH: when $[\text{H}^+]$ changes. Clock timing: measure time to a visible end-point.

Learning goals
  • Match each technique to the property it tracks.
  • Justify a choice for a given reaction.
Examiner tips
  • Pressure only works if the total moles of gas change; conductivity only if the ionic strength changes.
5. The iodine clock — Harcourt–Esson (CP 9b)
$\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 2\text{I}^- + 2\text{H}^+ \rightarrow \text{I}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$
$\text{scavenger: } \text{I}_2 + 2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{I}^- + \text{S}_4\text{O}_6^{2-}$

A small, fixed amount of thiosulfate + starch is added. Any $\text{I}_2$ made is instantly reduced back to $\text{I}^-$ until the thiosulfate runs out; then free $\text{I}_2$ turns the starch blue-black. Time to colour change $t$ gives $1/t \propto$ initial rate. Vary one reactant per set of runs.

Learning goals
  • Explain the scavenger role of thiosulfate.
  • Relate $1/t$ to the initial rate and deduce orders.
  • State why thiosulfate must be small and constant.
Examiner tips
  • If thiosulfate were large, reactant concentrations would change significantly during $t$ — the "initial rate" approximation breaks down.
6. Iodine–propanone reaction (CP 9a)
$\text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_3 + \text{I}_2 \xrightarrow{\text{H}^+} \text{CH}_3\text{COCH}_2\text{I} + \text{HI}$

Follow the disappearance of brown $\text{I}_2$. Titrimetric: withdraw timed samples into excess $\text{NaHCO}_3$ (quenches the acid catalyst), then titrate remaining $\text{I}_2$ with thiosulfate. Colorimetric: monitor the fading brown colour directly. Result: first order in propanone and $\text{H}^+$, zero order in $\text{I}_2$.

Learning goals
  • Describe both the titrimetric and colorimetric routes.
  • Explain why quenching with $\text{NaHCO}_3$ stops the reaction.
Examiner tips
  • Removing $\text{H}^+$ (the catalyst) by neutralisation is what quenches this reaction — a nice one-mark explanation.
7. Finding $E_a$ (CP 10)
$\text{measure rate at several } T \rightarrow \ln k \text{ vs } 1/T \rightarrow \text{gradient} = -E_a/R$

Run the same reaction (e.g. $\text{Mg} + \text{HCl}$, or an iodine clock) at 4–6 temperatures. Determine $k$ (or use $1/t$) at each. Plot $\ln k$ against $1/T$; the gradient $= -E_a/R$, so $E_a = -\text{gradient}\times 8.31\ \text{J mol}^{-1}\text{K}^{-1}$, then convert to kJ.

Learning goals
  • Outline the multi-temperature procedure.
  • Extract $E_a$ from the gradient with correct units.
Examiner tips
  • Use a water bath for stable, known temperatures; record $T$ in K.

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Methods & Practicals
Trap 1: Mass loss vs gas syringe

Mass loss suits a heavy gas ($\text{CO}_2$). For $\text{H}_2$ the mass change is tiny — use a gas syringe. Justify by the gas's molar mass.

Trap 2: Starch timing in titration

Add starch near the end-point (when the solution is pale yellow). Adding it at the start gives a stubborn iodine–starch complex and a poor end-point.

Trap 3: Clock thiosulfate amount

Thiosulfate must be small and constant across runs. Too much and reactant concentrations drift during the timed interval, invalidating $1/t$ as an initial rate.

Trap 4: Initial vs average rate

Orders come from initial rates (tangent at $t=0$ or clock $1/t$). An average rate over a long interval underestimates the true starting rate.

Engine 5 · Learning Suite

Homogeneous & Heterogeneous Catalysis (Spec 11.1 ix, 11.11)

5 concepts mapped
1. Homogeneous vs heterogeneous catalysts
$\text{homogeneous: same phase}\qquad \text{heterogeneous: different phase}$

A homogeneous catalyst is in the same phase as the reactants (e.g. $\text{H}^+_{(aq)}$ catalysing an aqueous reaction). A heterogeneous catalyst is in a different phase — usually a solid with gaseous or liquid reactants (e.g. Fe in the Haber process).

Learning goals
  • Classify a catalyst as homogeneous or heterogeneous from the phases.
  • Give one example of each.
Examiner tips
  • $\text{H}^+$ in propanone iodination is a classic homogeneous catalyst — it appears in the rate equation.
2. How a solid (heterogeneous) catalyst works
$\text{adsorption} \rightarrow \text{bond weakening} \rightarrow \text{reaction} \rightarrow \text{desorption}$

Reactant molecules adsorb onto the solid surface, which weakens their bonds and lowers $E_a$. Reaction occurs on the surface, then products desorb. The catalyst provides an alternative pathway and is unchanged at the end.

Learning goals
  • Describe the adsorb → react → desorb sequence.
  • Explain the lowering of $E_a$ in terms of bond weakening on the surface.
Examiner tips
  • Use adsorption (surface attachment), not absorption (soaking in). This word choice is marked.
3. Industrial heterogeneous catalysts
$\text{Fe (Haber)}\;\cdot\;\text{Ni (hydrogenation)}\;\cdot\;\text{V}_2\text{O}_5\text{ (Contact)}\;\cdot\;\text{Pt/Rh (converter)}$

Haber process: Fe makes ammonia from $\text{N}_2 + 3\text{H}_2$. Hydrogenation: Ni adds $\text{H}_2$ to alkenes. Contact process: $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$ oxidises $\text{SO}_2$ to $\text{SO}_3$. Catalytic converter: Pt/Rh/Pd convert CO and NO to $\text{CO}_2$ and $\text{N}_2$.

Learning goals
  • Match each industrial process to its solid catalyst.
  • State the reactants and products for each.
Examiner tips
  • Don't confuse the Contact process catalyst ($\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$) with the Haber catalyst (Fe).
4. Catalyst, $E_a$ and equilibrium
$\text{lowers }E_a\;\cdot\;\text{speeds forward \& reverse equally}\;\cdot\;K\text{ unchanged}$

A catalyst lowers the activation energy for both the forward and reverse reactions by the same amount. Equilibrium is reached faster, but the position of equilibrium and the value of $K$ are unchanged. It does not alter $\Delta H$.

Learning goals
  • State that a catalyst affects rate, not equilibrium position or $K$.
  • Explain the equal lowering of forward and reverse $E_a$.
Examiner tips
  • "Increases the yield" is wrong for a catalyst — it only speeds attainment of the same yield.
5. Homogeneous catalysis examples
$\text{H}^+ \text{ (propanone iodination, ester hydrolysis)}\;\cdot\;\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}^{3+}\text{ (persulfate–iodide)}$

In homogeneous catalysis the catalyst mixes with the reactants in one phase. $\text{H}^+_{(aq)}$ catalyses the iodination of propanone and ester hydrolysis. Transition-metal ions in solution (e.g. $\text{Fe}^{2+}/\text{Fe}^{3+}$) catalyse redox reactions by shuttling oxidation states.

Learning goals
  • Recognise $\text{H}^+$ as a homogeneous catalyst in Topic 11 reactions.
  • Explain why a homogeneous catalyst can appear in the rate equation.
Examiner tips
  • Because it is in the same phase and in the RDS, a homogeneous catalyst shows up as a concentration term (e.g. $[\text{H}^+]$).

⚡ High-Yield Trap Pairs — Catalysis
Trap 1: Adsorption vs absorption

Adsorption: molecules stick to the surface (heterogeneous catalysis). Absorption: a substance is taken into the bulk. Only the first word earns the mark.

Trap 2: Catalyst vs temperature

A catalyst lowers $E_a$. Raising temperature does not change $E_a$ — it raises the fraction of molecules above it. Never mix the two explanations.

Trap 3: Rate vs yield

A catalyst increases the rate, not the yield or $K$. It speeds forward and reverse equally, so the equilibrium composition is identical.

Trap 4: Fe vs $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$

Haber = Fe. Contact = $\text{V}_2\text{O}_5$. Hydrogenation = Ni. Swapping these is a classic multiple-choice distractor.

🧮 Method A — Two-Temperature Calculator

Uses $E_a = -\text{gradient} \times R$. Enter the gradient of your $\ln k$ vs $1/T$ plot (it will be negative, in K).

📝 Worked Example (Method A)

A reaction has $k_1 = 2.5\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $25^\circ\text{C}$ and $k_2 = 9.8\times10^{-3}\ \text{s}^{-1}$ at $45^\circ\text{C}$. Find $E_a$.

$T_1 = 298\ \text{K},\; T_2 = 318\ \text{K}$
$\ln\dfrac{9.8\times10^{-3}}{2.5\times10^{-3}} = \ln 3.92 = 1.366$
$\dfrac{1}{T_1} - \dfrac{1}{T_2} = \dfrac{1}{298} - \dfrac{1}{318} = 2.110\times10^{-4}\ \text{K}^{-1}$
$E_a = \dfrac{1.366 \times 8.31}{2.110\times10^{-4}} = 5.38\times10^{4}\ \text{J mol}^{-1} = \mathbf{53.8\ \text{kJ mol}^{-1}}$

  • Orders come from experiment, never from stoichiometric coefficients.
  • "Order of reaction" = overall order (the sum).
  • Zero order means "not in the RDS", not "not in the mechanism".
  • Units of $k$ depend on overall order — always give them.
  • Constant half-life ⇒ first order; measure two or more to prove it.
  • $t_{1/2}=0.693/k$ is first order only.
  • Second-order rate–concentration graph is a curve through the origin.
  • Initial rate = tangent at $t=0$, not a chord.
  • In the initial-rate method, vary one concentration; keep $T$ and the rest constant.

  • Quench samples before titrating, or the reaction continues.
