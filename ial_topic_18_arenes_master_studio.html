⚠️ PEF Reminders — Principal Examiner Feedback (2019–2025) from chemistry-wiki wchNN-examiner-insights
  • Benzene delocalisation evidence: hydrogenation enthalpy of benzene is LESS exothermic than the theoretical 1,3,5-triene (compare to the triene, NOT cyclohexene); X-ray: all C–C equal length, LONGER than the C=C of cyclohexene; bromination needs a CATALYST and is substitution, NOT addition (WCH15).
  • Electrophilic substitution mechanism: curly arrow #1 from the RING of delocalised electrons → electrophile (NO₂⁺); arrow #2 from the C–H bond (not H atom); “horseshoe” open end faces the tetrahedral carbon; positive charge inside the horseshoe; regenerate the catalyst (H₂SO₄) with an equation (WCH15).
  • Nitration: nitrating mixture = concentrated HNO₃ + concentrated H₂SO₄; electrophile NO₂⁺ (nitronium) (WCH15).
  • Phenol reactivity: lone pair on the OXYGEN of –OH overlaps into the ring → increased electron density → reacts more readily (no catalyst, room temperature) than benzene; must say “lone pair of electrons from the oxygen”; phenol directs ortho/para (WCH15).
  • Azo dyes: cold diazonium salt (0–10°C, unstable — breaks down to phenol) + coupling with phenol; N≡N → N=N in product (WCH15).
  • Grignard with aromatic ring: brominate ring (with catalyst), form R–Mg–Br (Mg on carbon, not Br on ring), react with C=O/CO₂, HYDROLYSE the salt separately; dry ether essential (WCH15).
  • Nitro → amine reduction: Sn/concentrated HCl (tin is a REDUCING agent, not catalyst); don't add NaOH in same step; don't use LiAlH₄ here (WCH15).
  • Benzene with cyanide is incorrect (benzene doesn't do that) — but transfer-error could be awarded if the nitrile was then hydrolysed to benzoic acid (WCH15).

Spec 18.1 Evidence for structure

Evidence for the structure & stability of benzene

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Thermochemical: hydrogenation of benzene is −208 kJ mol⁻¹, far less exothermic than 3 × cyclohexene (3 × −120 = −360) → benzene is ~152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than a Kekulé structure.
  • X-ray diffraction: all six C–C bonds are equal length (0.139 nm) — between C–C (0.154) and C=C (0.134); a Kekulé structure would alternate.
  • Infrared: no absorption for a localised C=C stretch; consistent with delocalised bonding.
  • State that benzene is planar, hexagonal, with bond angles of 120°.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Quote the 152 kJ mol⁻¹ stabilisation and the equal bond lengths — vague “more stable” answers lose marks. The hypothetical value is 3 × cyclohexene, not “three double bonds”. Valid evidence = thermochemical, IR, X-ray diffraction — mass spectrometry is NOT evidence for delocalisation (WCH15 Jun 2026 Q9).

Spec 18.2 Delocalised model

p-orbital overlap & the delocalised π system

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Each carbon is sp² hybridised: three σ bonds (two C–C, one C–H) in a plane at 120°.
  • Each carbon has one electron in a p-orbital perpendicular to the ring plane.
  • The six p-orbitals overlap sideways, above and below the plane, forming a delocalised π system of 6 electrons.
  • Represent benzene as a hexagon with an internal circle (delocalised model).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Say the p-orbitals overlap sideways / laterally, above and below the plane. “Electrons move freely” is too vague — say delocalised over all six carbons.

Spec 18.3 Benzene vs alkenes

Why benzene resists bromination (vs alkenes)

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Alkenes have a localised π bond with high electron density → readily polarise Br₂ → addition at room temperature, no catalyst.
  • Benzene’s π electrons are delocalised over six carbons → lower local electron density → cannot polarise Br₂ alone.
  • Benzene needs a halogen carrier (FeBr₃) to generate a strong electrophile, and undergoes substitution (preserving the ring) rather than addition.
  • Addition would destroy the delocalised system (energetically unfavourable); substitution restores it.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The mark is for delocalised vs localised electron density — and for substitution preserving the stable ring. “Benzene is unreactive” is wrong; it is resistant to addition.

Spec 18.4 Reactions of benzene

The five reactions of benzene

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • i. Combustion in air: very smoky yellow flame (high C:H ratio).
  • ii. Bromine with FeBr₃ catalyst → bromobenzene + HBr.
  • iii. Conc. HNO₃ + conc. H₂SO₄ (50–60 °C) → nitrobenzene.
  • iv. Fuming H₂SO₄ → benzenesulfonic acid.
  • v. Friedel–Crafts: halogenoalkane or acyl chloride with anhydrous AlCl₃ → alkylbenzene / aromatic ketone.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Conditions are mark points: the catalyst (FeBr₃ / AlCl₃), the nitrating mixture, and the 50–60 °C temperature all score. See the full table in Tab 4.

Spec 18.5 Mechanism

Electrophilic substitution mechanism

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Step 1 — generate the electrophile: Br⁺ (from Br₂/FeBr₃), NO₂⁺ (from HNO₃/H₂SO₄), R⁺ or RCO⁺ (from RCl/RCOCl + AlCl₃).
  • Step 2 — electrophile attacks the π system → carbocation (arenium) intermediate; the ring temporarily loses aromaticity.
  • Step 3 — loss of H⁺ (taken by FeBr₄⁻ / HSO₄⁻ / AlCl₄⁻) → aromatic ring restored; catalyst regenerated.
  • Draw the mechanism for halogenation, nitration and Friedel–Crafts (Tab 3 player).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Examiners want the electrophile generation equation AND the catalyst regeneration step (2023/2024 papers). Forgetting to regenerate the catalyst is the classic lost mark. Nitration needs conc H₂SO₄ + conc HNO₃ to make NO₂⁺ — HCl will not (it gives diazonium elsewhere); Friedel–Crafts uses AlCl₃/FeCl₃ generating e.g. CH₂ClCO⁺ (not +CH₂COCl). Paper cues: WCH15 Jun 2026 Q11c nitration, Q14 benzene synthesis.

Spec 18.6 Phenol

Phenol + bromine water — and why it is so easy

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Phenol reacts with bromine water at room temperature, no catalyst → 2,4,6-tribromophenol (white precipitate) + decolourisation.
  • Explain the ease: the oxygen lone pair delocalises into the ring, raising electron density (activating the ring).
  • Substitution occurs at the 2, 4 and 6 positions (ortho/para directing).
  • Contrast with benzene, which needs FeBr₃ and gives only monosubstitution.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Two observations score: decolourisation AND white precipitate. The explanation must mention the oxygen lone pair delocalising into the ring — “phenol is more reactive” alone is not enough. Paper cue: WCH15 Jun 2026 Q15 (phenol vs hex-1-ene) — both react with electrophiles without a catalyst; hex-1-ene = addition (localised π), phenol = substitution (delocalised ring, kinetically stable).

Also expected: common uses of the compounds mentioned (e.g. nitrobenzene → dyes/phenylamine; sulfonic acids → detergents; phenol → resins/disinfectants; toluene → TNT). Out of scope here: orientation rules for substituted benzenes beyond 2,4,6-activation by –OH, and full arene synthesis pathways (covered in later organic topics).
Spec 18.1 – 18.3

Evidence for the Structure & Stability of Benzene

Three independent lines of evidence disprove the Kekulé model, plus the thermochemical proof of delocalisation stabilisation.

🔥 Thermochemical

Hydrogenation of benzene releases −208 kJ mol⁻¹, not the −360 kJ mol⁻¹ expected for three isolated C=C (3 × cyclohexene = 3 × −120). Benzene is 152 kJ mol⁻¹ more stable than Kekulé predicted.

📐 X-ray Diffraction

All six C–C bonds are the same length (0.139 nm) — intermediate between C–C (0.154 nm) and C=C (0.134 nm). Kekulé would require alternating long/short bonds.

〰️ Infrared

No absorption corresponding to a localised C=C stretch — consistent with delocalised π bonding rather than three discrete double bonds.

Hydrogenation Enthalpy Comparison — the 152 kJ mol⁻¹ proof

The real benzene curve sits 152 kJ mol⁻¹ above the hypothetical cyclohexatriene value — that gap is the delocalisation (resonance) stabilisation energy.

The delocalised model (18.2)

  • Each C is sp²: three σ bonds in a plane, 120°.
  • One electron per C in a p-orbital ⊥ to the plane.
  • Six p-orbitals overlap sideways above & below the plane → one delocalised π cloud holding 6 electrons.
  • Drawn as a hexagon with an internal circle.

Spec 18.5

Electrophilic Substitution — Step-by-Step Mechanism Player

Choose a reaction, then walk the three stages: electrophile generation → arenium intermediate → aromaticity restored & catalyst regenerated.

Electrophile: $\text{Br}^+$ 1 / 3

Step 1 — Generation of the electrophile

Why substitution, not addition? The electrophile takes electrons from the delocalised π cloud, forming a carbocation intermediate that has lost aromaticity. Losing H⁺ (rather than adding a nucleophile) restores the stable 6-electron delocalised ring — that energetic payoff is why substitution wins.

Spec 18.6 + acidity

Phenol — Activated Ring & Acidic OH

The –OH group both activates the ring toward electrophilic substitution and makes the O–H bond acidic.

Why the ring is activated (2,4,6-directing)

A lone pair on the oxygen delocalises into the benzene ring, increasing the π electron density — especially at the 2, 4 and 6 positions. The ring is far more attractive to electrophiles than benzene itself.

Bromination: bromine water, room temperature, no catalyst → immediate decolourisation + white precipitate of 2,4,6-tribromophenol.

$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 3\text{Br}_2 \rightarrow 2,4,6\text{-C}_6\text{H}_2\text{Br}_3\text{OH} + 3\text{HBr}$
BenzenePhenol

Why the OH is acidic (phenol vs ethanol)

When phenol loses H⁺, the resulting phenoxide ion is stabilised — the negative charge delocalises into the ring. Ethanol’s ethoxide ion has no such stabilisation, so ethanol does not donate H⁺ to NaOH.

$2\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + 2\text{Na} \rightarrow 2\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}^-\text{Na}^+ + \text{H}_2$ (effervescence)
