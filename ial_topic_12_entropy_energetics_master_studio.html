Spec 12.1 – 12.2 Why ΔH fails · what S is

Enthalpy is not the boss — entropy is disorder

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Argue from evidence: endothermic reactions can be spontaneous at room temperature (NH₄NO₃ dissolving; Ba(OH)₂·8H₂O + NH₄Cl), so ΔH alone cannot decide whether a reaction occurs.
  • Define entropy as a measure of the disorder of a system.
  • Describe disorder in two ways: random dispersal of molecules (positions) and dispersal of energy quanta between molecules.
  • Link more possible arrangements (more ways to spread out) → higher entropy.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Exothermic = spontaneous” is a guaranteed lost mark — quote an endothermic counter-example. Say “dispersal of energy quanta”, not just “energy spreads out”, for the full definition. When explaining a negative ΔS sign, never write bare “entropy decreases” — the MS demands order/disorder or particle numbers (e.g. “gas → solid, fewer ways to arrange the particles”). Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q20a gave ΔSsystem = −507.6 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ for a gas→solid step.

Spec 12.3 – 12.4 S vs T & state · direction

How entropy changes — and which way things naturally go

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State that entropy of a substance increases with temperature (more energy quanta to disperse).
  • Order: $S$(solid) < $S$(liquid) << $S$(gas); a perfect crystal at 0 K has zero entropy.
  • Note standard molar entropies $S^\circ$ are absolute values (unlike enthalpies, which are changes).
  • Interpret the natural direction of change as the direction of increasing total entropy — e.g. a gas spreads spontaneously through a room and never re-collects.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

It is the total entropy (universe) that must increase — a system’s entropy can fall (freezing water) as long as the surroundings gain more. The gas-diffusion example is a favourite 1-mark “explain the natural direction” stem.

Spec 12.5 Why ΔS happens

Three situations that change entropy

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Changes of state: solid → liquid → gas; the big jump is vaporisation (molecules escape the liquid entirely).
  • Dissolving an ionic lattice: ordered crystal → free mobile ions usually raises entropy, but ordering of water around ions can make ΔS negative for some salts (e.g. CaSO₄).
  • Reactions changing gas moles: more moles of gas on the product side → large positive ΔS; fewer → negative. This is usually the dominant factor.
  • Predict the sign of ΔS for unfamiliar reactions using these three rules.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Count gas moles only — solids and liquids barely count. CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) is positive even with two solids, because a gas appears. Practise in Engine A’s sign predictor.

Spec 12.6 – 12.7 ΔS total · ΔS system

The two entropy changes and how to calculate ΔSsystem

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • State: $\Delta S_\text{total} = \Delta S_\text{system} + \Delta S_\text{surroundings}$ — system = the chemicals; surroundings = everything else.
  • Calculate $\Delta S_\text{system} = \Sigma S^\circ(\text{products}) - \Sigma S^\circ(\text{reactants})$ from tabulated standard entropies.
  • Multiply each $S^\circ$ by its balancing coefficient and keep state symbols in mind.
  • Units throughout: J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Products minus reactants — the same order as Hess, but with absolute $S^\circ$ values (no elements-equal-zero rule here; elements have real entropies).

Spec 12.8 ΔS surroundings

ΔSsurroundings = −ΔH/T and the unit trap

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Use $\Delta S_\text{surroundings} = -\dfrac{\Delta H}{T}$ with $T$ in kelvin.
  • Reason it: exothermic reactions heat the surroundings → more dispersal of energy quanta → positive ΔSsurr.
  • See the $T$ in the denominator: the same ΔH creates a smaller ΔSsurr at high temperature.
  • Combine to get $\Delta S_\text{total}$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

ΔH is tabulated in kJ, ΔS in J — convert ΔH × 1000 before dividing, or your ΔSsurr is 1000× too small. This single slip costs more marks than any concept in the topic. The recurring 3-mark temperature argument: M1 Ssurr becomes less negative / smaller in magnitude as T rises (dividing −ΔH by a larger T) — not bare “smaller”; M2 ΔSsystem barely changes with T; M3 so ΔStotal increases. Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q20a(iv) — ΔSsurr = +4724.8 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, ΔStotal = +4217 J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹.

Spec 12.9 – 12.10 Feasibility · T*

Feasibility, the crossover temperature, and the ΔG option

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • A reaction is feasible when $\Delta S_\text{total} > 0$ — even if one component is negative (the other must outweigh it).
  • Explain endothermic spontaneity: a large positive ΔSsystem beats a negative ΔSsurroundings.
  • Explain temperature dependence: higher $T$ shrinks |ΔSsurr|, so endothermic reactions become feasible when hot.
  • Calculate the feasibility temperature from $\Delta S_\text{total} = 0$ → $T = \dfrac{\Delta H}{\Delta S_\text{system}}$ (ΔH in J!).
  • Know $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system}$ is allowed in answers but not required.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Mark schemes (Jan 2026, Oct 2025) award T* via ΔStotal = 0. “Feasible” ≠ “fast” — say nothing about rate unless asked. Test all four sign combinations in Engine B’s workbench. T* = ΔH/ΔSsys with ΔH in J — a 0.273 vs 273 K mix-up is a 2/3 mark call. Remember ΔStotal = R ln K: if K < 1, ΔStotal < 0 → equilibrium lies left (reactants predominate) — state the position consequence, not just “K is small”.

Spec 12.11 Stability types

Thermodynamic vs kinetic stability

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Thermodynamic stability: judged by ΔStotal (or ΔG) — will it happen at all?
  • Kinetic stability: judged by activation energy — how fast?
  • Classic case: diamond → graphite is thermodynamically feasible but kinetically stable (huge $E_a$) at room temperature.
  • Mg ribbon in air: feasible (very negative ΔG) yet sits unchanged until ignited — kinetic barrier.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

“Thermodynamically unstable but kinetically stable” is the exact phrase examiners look for. Never write “stable” alone — say which kind.

12B · Lattice Energy
Spec 12.12 – 12.19
Spec 12.12 Five definitions

The definitions that Born–Haber questions assume

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Lattice energy: energy required to separate one mole of an ionic solid into its gaseous ions (endothermic, + in IAL convention).
  • ΔfH: enthalpy change when one mole of compound forms from its elements in standard states (100 kPa, stated T).
  • ΔatH: one mole of gaseous atoms from the element in its standard state (always endothermic).
  • First IE: energy to remove one mole of electrons from one mole of gaseous atoms.
  • First EA: enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous atoms each gains one electron (exothermic for F, Cl; second EA is endothermic — e.g. O⁻ → O²⁻).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Every definition must say “one mole” and name the states (gaseous ions). Some data books give lattice energy as the exothermic formation — check the sign convention in the question before calculating. MS keyword discipline: lattice energy = heat released when gaseous ions form 1 mol solid — REJECT “substance”/“energy required”; ΔhydH must say gaseous ions (not atoms/molecules) dissolving to infinite dilution — REJECT “1 mol of water”.

Spec 12.13 Born–Haber cycles

Construct Born–Haber cycles and calculate with them

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Build the cycle: elements → gaseous atoms (ΔatH) → gaseous ions (IE, EA) → ionic solid (−LE), with ΔfH as the direct route.
  • Apply Hess: $\Delta_f H = \Sigma\Delta_\text{at} H + \Sigma IE + \Sigma EA - LE$, so $LE = \Sigma\Delta_\text{at} H + \Sigma IE + \Sigma EA - \Delta_f H$.
  • Handle divalent compounds: two ionisation energies, doubled atomisation/EA for X₂ (MgCl₂, MgO).
  • Draw energy-level diagrams with endothermic arrows up, exothermic down.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Forgetting to double ΔatH and EA for the halogen (Cl₂ → 2Cl) is the classic MgCl₂ slip. EA₂ for oxygen is endothermic (+) — O⁻ repels the second electron. Solve cycles live in Engine C. MS error menu (Na₂O worked): wrong sign on formation, forgetting ×2 for both atomisation AND ionisation, sign-flipped EA, or halved atomisation — two errors → 0 overall; units penalised once. Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q20b(vi) — LE = −4690 − 1092 − (−311) = −5471 kJ mol⁻¹.

Spec 12.14 – 12.15 Covalency evidence

Experimental vs theoretical lattice energy → covalent character

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Theoretical LE: from electrostatic theory assuming a perfect ionic model — point charges, spherical ions, no covalency.
  • Experimental LE: from the Born–Haber cycle (real thermochemical data).
  • Close agreement → ionic bonding; experimental value of greater magnitude → covalent character (extra electron sharing strengthens the lattice).
  • Explain via polarisation: a small, highly charged cation distorts a large anion’s electron cloud.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Name both players: “small, highly charged cation polarises the large anion”. Compare pairs (NaCl vs AgCl; LiF vs LiI) — the gap grows with anion size and cation charge density. MS phrasing: experimental LE more negative / more exothermic than theoretical (not bare “greater/less”), because the theoretical model is pure ionic (point charges / perfect spheres / no distortion). Paper cue: WCH14 Jun 2026 Q20b(iii) — ΔhydH(Al³⁺) > ΔhydH(Cl⁻) via higher charge, not “polarising power”.

Spec 12.16 – 12.17 Solution cycles

ΔsolH, ΔhydH and the dissolving energy cycle

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • ΔsolH: enthalpy change when one mole of an ionic solid dissolves to give aqueous ions (can be + or −).
  • ΔhydH: enthalpy change when one mole of gaseous ions becomes aqueous ions — always exothermic (ion–dipole attraction to water).
  • Build the cycle: solid → gaseous ions (+LE) → aqueous ions (ΣΔhydH).
  • Calculate: $\Delta_\text{sol} H = LE + \Delta_\text{hyd} H(\text{cation}) + \Delta_\text{hyd} H(\text{anion})$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Sign discipline: LE enters as a positive (dissociation) term; both hydration values are negative. A positive ΔsolH does not mean insoluble — entropy can still drive dissolving (NaCl!). For “why no temperature change?” (NaCl, ΔsolH ≈ +1 kJ mol⁻¹): the change is too small / slightly endothermic for the thermometer (±0.5 °C graduations too large) — REJECT any “temperature increase”. Solubility (3-mark QWC): name ΔhydH, compare its size to lattice energy, and note ΔStotal can still drive dissolving even if ΔsolH > 0.

Spec 12.18 – 12.19 Trends · solubility

Charge/radius effects and predicting solubility

🎯 Key Learning Goals:
  • Smaller ions and higher charges → more exothermic ΔhydH (stronger ion–dipole) and larger lattice energy (stronger electrostatic attraction).
  • Predict solubility: dissolving is feasible when $\Delta S_\text{total(sol)} = \Delta S_\text{sys} + (-\Delta_\text{sol} H / T) > 0$.
  • Discuss Unit 2 trends: Group 2 hydroxides more soluble down the group; Group 2 sulfates less soluble down the group.
  • Reason the split: with small OH⁻ the lattice energy change dominates down the group; with large SO₄²⁻ the hydration energy change dominates.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Given-data questions want the calculation (ΔStotal sign), then the trend discussion. Do not write “like dissolves like” — use energy + entropy language. Explore real salts in Engine E. Group 2 solubility: hydroxides more soluble down the group because ΔStotal gets less negative / more positive Ca(OH)₂ → Ba(OH)₂ (ALLOW reverse argument). Calorimetry of dissolving (WCH16): to get a bigger ΔT use more solute / less water — REJECT “repeat”, “lid/insulation” (reaction calorimetry, not this), “distilled water”; when ΔT is small the thermometer is the limiting instrument.

Out of scope in this studio: Topic 13 Chemical Equilibria ($K_c$ expressions — next topic), statistical thermodynamics (Boltzmann equation), and detailed transition-metal cycles. $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system}$ is permitted in answers but not required — the studio teaches the ΔStotal route as primary.
Engine A · Syllabus 12.1–12.5

Entropy Concepts Suite

5 concept cards + sign predictor
1. Endothermic yet spontaneous (12.1)
ΔH > 0 but still happens ⇒ something else drives reactions: entropy

Evidence: NH₄NO₃ dissolving in water (instant cold packs, ΔH ≈ +26 kJ mol⁻¹); Ba(OH)₂·8H₂O(s) + 2NH₄Cl(s) → BaCl₂ + 2NH₃ + 10H₂O — temperature plunges below 0 °C yet the reaction runs, releasing ammonia gas.

If enthalpy alone controlled reactions, every spontaneous change would be exothermic. It isn’t.

Learning goals
  • State that enthalpy changes alone do not control whether reactions occur.
  • Quote an endothermic spontaneous example in any “why is entropy needed?” answer.
Examiner tips
  • “All spontaneous reactions are exothermic” — always false; one counter-example ends the claim.
2. Entropy = disorder (12.2)

Definition: entropy measures the disorder of a system — in terms of the random dispersal of molecules (where they can be) and of energy quanta between molecules (how energy is shared out).

More ways to arrange the molecules and energy → higher entropy. A gas has vastly more arrangements than a crystal.

Learning goals
  • Give both halves of the definition: molecules AND energy quanta.
  • Symbol and units: $S$, J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
Examiner tips
  • “Randomness” alone is thin — “dispersal of energy quanta” is the mark-scheme phrase that separates full answers.
3. Entropy vs temperature and state (12.3)

Temperature: hotter → more energy quanta available to disperse → $S$ rises.

State: $S$(s) < $S$(l) << $S$(g). The vaporisation jump dwarfs melting.

Zero point: a perfect crystal at 0 K has $S = 0$ — so standard molar entropies are absolute values, unlike enthalpies which only ever come as changes.

Learning goals
  • Rank entropies of the same substance in three states.
  • Explain why $S^\circ$ values can be tabulated as absolute numbers.
Examiner tips
  • Do not say elements have zero entropy — only perfect crystals at 0 K do.
4. The natural direction of change (12.4)

Changes naturally go in the direction of increasing total entropy (system + surroundings).

Iconic example: open a gas jar in a room and the gas spreads out — it never spontaneously re-collects into the jar, though nothing forbids it energetically. The dispersed state simply has overwhelmingly more arrangements.

Learning goals
  • Interpret any one-way process (diffusion, heat flow, mixing) as total-entropy increase.
  • Stress “total” — the system alone may lose entropy.
Examiner tips
  • Water freezing at −10 °C: system entropy falls, but the surroundings gain more — total still rises. A favourite “explain” stem.
5. Why entropy changes occur (12.5)

i. Changes of state: melting frees molecules a little; vaporisation frees them completely (big +ΔS).

ii. Dissolving an ionic lattice: crystal → mobile ions usually +ΔS, but water molecules ordering around small/charged ions can tip it negative (CaSO₄, some carbonates).

iii. Gas-mole changes: the heavyweight. More gas moles on the right → large +ΔS; fewer → −ΔS. Solids/liquids barely register.

Learning goals
  • Classify any given ΔS by which of the three causes dominates.
  • Predict signs fast: gas count first, state change second, dissolving third.
Examiner tips
  • CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g): positive despite two solids — a gas appears.
  • H₂(g) + Cl₂(g) → 2HCl(g): ΔS ≈ 0 — equal gas moles.

Engine B · Syllabus 12.6–12.11

ΔS Calculations & Feasibility Workbench

5 cards + live ΔS-total curve
1. System + surroundings = total (12.6)
$\Delta S_\text{total} = \Delta S_\text{system} + \Delta S_\text{surroundings}$

System = the reacting chemicals. Surroundings = everything else (the beaker, the air, the universe beyond the flask).

Second law: a change is feasible only if the total entropy increases. Either part may be negative — the sum decides.

Learning goals
  • Write the equation from memory and define both terms.
  • State the feasibility criterion: ΔS_total > 0.
Examiner tips
  • Never judge feasibility from ΔS_system alone — half the class does.
2. Calculating ΔS system (12.7)
$\Delta S_\text{system} = \Sigma S^\circ(\text{products}) - \Sigma S^\circ(\text{reactants})$

Worked: for N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃ with S° = 192, 131, 193 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹: ΔS_sys = 2(193) − [192 + 3(131)] = 386 − 585 = −199 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹. Negative — 4 gas moles became 2.

Learning goals
  • Apply coefficients; keep products-minus-reactants order.
  • Sanity-check the sign against the gas-mole count afterwards.
Examiner tips
  • Elements are NOT zero here — every species contributes its real S°.
3. ΔS surroundings = −ΔH/T (12.8)
$\Delta S_\text{surr} = -\dfrac{\Delta H}{T}$  ·  T in kelvin · ΔH in J

Why the minus sign: exothermic (ΔH < 0) reactions dump heat into the surroundings → more dispersal of energy quanta there → positive ΔS_surr.

Why ÷T: the same heat makes a bigger mess in a cold surroundings than a hot one — like shouting in a library vs a concert.

Learning goals
  • Convert ΔH from kJ to J before dividing (×1000).
  • Explain both the sign and the temperature dependence in words.
Examiner tips
  • The kJ/J slip is the single most common lost mark in Unit 4 physical chemistry. Convert first, always.
4. Feasibility and the crossover temperature (12.9–12.10)

Four cases: exo + ΔS_sys>0 → always feasible · endo + ΔS_sys<0 → never · endo + ΔS_sys>0 → feasible above T* · exo + ΔS_sys<0 → feasible below T*.

Crossover: set ΔS_total = 0 → $T^* = \dfrac{\Delta H}{\Delta S_\text{system}}$ (ΔH in J). CaCO₃ decomposition: T* = 178000/160 ≈ 1112 K (839 °C) — why lime kilns run hot.

ΔG route (allowed, not required): $\Delta G = \Delta H - T\Delta S_\text{system}$; feasible when ΔG < 0 — same physics, different bookkeeping.

Learning goals
  • Classify any reaction into the four cases from the two signs.
  • Calculate T* and state “feasible above/below” correctly.
Examiner tips
  • Mark schemes award T* via ΔS_total = 0. “Feasible” says nothing about speed — don’t volunteer rate comments.
5. Thermodynamic vs kinetic stability (12.11)

Thermodynamic: is the change feasible (ΔS_total > 0)? Kinetic: how fast — set by the activation energy.

Diamond → graphite: feasible at room temperature, yet diamonds persist for geological time — the rearrangement barrier is enormous. Thermodynamically unstable, kinetically stable.

Mg + O₂: hugely feasible, but the ribbon waits for a flame to supply the activation energy.

Learning goals
  • Distinguish the two stabilities and name the controlling factor for each (ΔS_total vs Eₐ).
Examiner tips
  • Use the full phrase “kinetically stable because of a high activation energy” — “stable” alone scores nothing.

Engine C · Syllabus 12.12–12.13

Born–Haber Cycle Workbench

4 compounds · energy diagram
1. Five definitions you must own (12.12)
Lattice energy: separate 1 mol ionic solid → gaseous ions (endothermic, +)

ΔfH: 1 mol compound from elements in standard states. ΔatH: 1 mol gaseous atoms from the element (always +). IE₁: remove 1 mol e⁻ from 1 mol gaseous atoms (+). EA₁: 1 mol gaseous atoms each gain 1 e⁻ (− for F/Cl).

EA₂ is endothermic: O⁻(g) + e⁻ → O²⁻(g) needs energy — an electron is forced onto a negative ion.

Learning goals
  • Recite all five with “one mole” and states included.
  • Know the sign of each: ΔatH +, IE +, EA₁ −, EA₂ +, LE + (IAL convention).
Examiner tips
  • Some questions define LE as the exothermic formation — read the given definition before writing signs.
2. Anatomy of a Born–Haber cycle (12.13)

Two routes from elements to ionic solid, equal by Hess’s law:

Direct: elements → solid ($\Delta_f H$, usually down). Indirect: elements → gaseous atoms (ΔatH, up) → gaseous ions (IE up, EA usually down) → solid (releases the lattice energy).

$\Delta_f H = \Sigma\Delta_\text{at} H + \Sigma IE + \Sigma EA - LE$  ⇒  $LE = \Sigma\Delta_\text{at} H + \Sigma IE + \Sigma EA - \Delta_f H$
Learning goals
  • Draw the energy-level diagram: endothermic arrows up, exothermic down, gaseous ions at the top.
  • Rearrange the Hess statement for any unknown, not just LE.
Examiner tips
  • Subtracting a negative ΔfH becomes addition — the most common arithmetic slip in these calculations.
3. Worked example: NaCl

Data (kJ mol⁻¹): ΔfH = −411 · ΔatH(Na) = +107 · IE₁(Na) = +496 · ΔatH(Cl) = +122 (½Cl₂ → Cl) · EA₁(Cl) = −346.

LE = 107 + 496 + 122 + (−346) − (−411) = +790 kJ mol⁻¹

Sanity check: lattice energies are large and endothermic in this convention — hundreds to thousands of kJ mol⁻¹. A small or negative answer means a sign went wrong.

Learning goals
  • Reproduce this calculation from the cycle without notes.
  • Remember ΔatH(Cl) is for ½Cl₂ → Cl — one Cl atom per NaCl.
Examiner tips
  • Show the full sum with signs — method marks survive arithmetic slips.
4. Divalent compounds: MgCl₂ and MgO

MgCl₂: two chlorines → double ΔatH(Cl) and double EA. Two charges on Mg → IE₁ + IE₂ (1451 kJ mol⁻¹ for the second — much larger, removing e⁻ from Mg⁺).

MgO adds a twist: oxygen’s second electron affinity is endothermic (O⁻ → O²⁻, ≈ +798 kJ mol⁻¹). The O²⁻ ion only exists because the huge lattice energy of Mg²⁺O²⁻ pays for it.

MgCl₂: LE = 148 + 2189 + 244 + (−692) − (−641) = +2530 kJ mol⁻¹
Learning goals
  • Build cycles for 2+/2− compounds without dropping doubled terms.
  • Explain why EA₂ is positive in words (repulsion onto a negative ion).
Examiner tips
  • Exam favourites: MgO, MgCl₂, NaF (Oct 2025, Jan 2024). Doubling errors are the marker’s gift — don’t wrap it.

Engine D · Syllabus 12.14–12.15

Covalent Character & Polarisation

6 compound comparator
1. The theoretical lattice energy (12.14)

Electrostatic theory calculates LE assuming a perfect ionic model: ions are point charges / perfect spheres with no distortion — pure electrostatic attraction, zero covalency.

It is a model — useful precisely because real compounds can deviate from it, and the deviation is measurable.

Learning goals
  • State the two assumptions: spherical ions, purely electrostatic (no covalent character).
Examiner tips
  • “Perfectly spherical ions” is a mark point on its own — don’t paraphrase it away.
2. Comparing experimental and theoretical LE (12.14)

Experimental LE comes from the Born–Haber cycle (real data). Theoretical LE comes from the ionic model.

Close agreement (NaCl: 790 vs 787) → bonding well described as ionic. Experimental value of greater magnitude (AgCl: 905 vs 770) → extra attraction the model can’t explain → covalent character (electron sharing strengthens the lattice).

Learning goals
  • Given two values, state the degree of covalent character and why.
  • Phrase it: “experimental more endothermic than theoretical (dissociation convention) ⇒ covalent character”.
Examiner tips
  • The conclusion must be comparative — quoting one number alone scores nothing.
3. Polarisation — the mechanism (12.15)

A small, highly charged cation (high charge density: Li⁺, Mg²⁺, Al³⁺, Ag⁺) attracts the anion’s electron cloud so strongly that it distorts (polarises) it. The cloud shifts toward the cation → partial electron sharing → covalent character.

The anion must be large and polarisable (I⁻ > Br⁻ > Cl⁻ > F⁻; S²⁻, O²⁻) — its outer electrons are loosely held.

Trends: for one cation, covalency grows down the halide group (LiF ionic → LiI covalent-leaning). For one anion, it grows with cation charge density (NaCl ≈ ionic vs AgCl covalent — hence silver halides’ odd solubilities).

Learning goals
  • Name both actors in every answer: polarising cation + polarisable anion.
  • Explain the distortion → sharing → stronger-than-predicted lattice chain.
Examiner tips
  • “The cation is polarised” reverses the roles — instant mark loss. Cations polarise; anions are polarised.

Solution Cycles & Solubility

7 salts · live ΔS verdict
1. Two more definitions (12.16)
ΔsolH: 1 mol solid → aqueous ions · ΔhydH: 1 mol gaseous ions → aqueous ions (always −)

ΔsolH can be positive (NH₄NO₃, KCl) or negative (NaOH, MgSO₄) — either sign is normal.

ΔhydH is always exothermic: gaseous ions form ion–dipole attractions with water molecules, releasing energy.

Learning goals
  • Write both definitions with “one mole” and states.
  • Explain why hydration is always exothermic (ion–dipole bonds forming).
Examiner tips
  • ΔhydH is per mole of IONS — Mg²⁺ and 2Cl⁻ contribute three separate hydration terms.
2. The solution energy cycle (12.17)

Two routes from solid to aqueous ions: rip the lattice apart first (endothermic, +LE), then hydrate the ions (exothermic).

$\Delta_\text{sol} H = LE + \Delta_\text{hyd} H(\text{cation}) + \Delta_\text{hyd} H(\text{anion})$

NaCl: 790 + (−406) + (−364) ≈ +20 kJ mol⁻¹ (representative data) — slightly endothermic, yet NaCl dissolves readily. Enthalpy is not the boss here either: the entropy gain of freeing the ions drives it.

Learning goals
  • Draw the three-level diagram: solid → gaseous ions → aqueous ions.
  • Rearrange for any unknown (LE from solubility data is a classic).
Examiner tips
  • Signs: LE enters positive; both hydration terms negative. A positive ΔsolH ≠ insoluble.
3. Ionic charge and radius effects (12.18)

Smaller ions / higher charge → stronger electrostatics → larger lattice energy AND more exothermic ΔhydH. Both move together — solubility depends on which moves faster.

Down Group 2 (Mg²⁺ → Ba²⁺): cation radius grows → LE and |ΔhydH| both shrink.

With the small OH⁻ anion, lattice energy falls faster → ΔsolH becomes more favourable → hydroxides more soluble down the group. With the large SO₄²⁻ anion, hydration energy falls faster → sulfates less soluble down the group.

Learning goals
  • State the charge/radius effect on LE and ΔhydH separately.
  • Explain why the two Group 2 trends run in opposite directions.
Examiner tips
  • Always say which term “dominates the change” — vague “gets weaker” answers score low.
4. Predicting solubility with entropy (12.19)
$\Delta S_\text{total(sol)} = \Delta S_\text{sys} + \dfrac{-\Delta_\text{sol} H}{T}$  — dissolving is feasible when > 0

BaSO₄: ΔS_sys ≈ −102 J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹ (ions order water more than the lattice loses) and ΔS_surr ≈ −87 → ΔS_total ≈ −189 → insoluble. NaCl: ΔS_sys ≈ +43, ΔS_surr ≈ −13 → +30 → soluble.

For large anions (SO₄²⁻), ΔS_sys of dissolving is strongly negative — the hydration ordering penalty — which is the entropy-side reason the sulfates resist dissolving.

Learning goals
  • Given ΔsolH and S° values, compute ΔS_total and conclude soluble/insoluble.
  • Link the Unit 2 hydroxide/sulfate trends to these energy terms.
Examiner tips
  • Conclusion first (“insoluble”), then the numbers. Marker-friendly order.

⚡ Entropy fundamentals (12.1–12.5)
  • Endothermic reactions can be spontaneous (NH₄NO₃ dissolving; Ba(OH)₂·8H₂O + NH₄Cl) ⇒ ΔH alone doesn’t decide.
  • Entropy = disorder: random dispersal of molecules and of energy quanta. Units J K⁻¹ mol⁻¹.
  • S rises with T; S(s) < S(l) << S(g); perfect crystal at 0 K: S = 0; S° values are absolute.
  • Natural direction = increasing total entropy (gas spreading through a room).
  • ΔS sign rules: gas-mole change dominates; then state change; then dissolving (usually +, sometimes −).

🏗️ Born–Haber method (12.13)

Route equality (Hess): ΔfH = ΣΔatH + ΣIE + ΣEA − LE ⇒ LE = ΣΔatH + ΣIE + ΣEA − ΔfH.

NaCl check: LE = 107 + 496 + 122 + (−346) − (−411) = +790 kJ mol⁻¹.

  • Divalent compounds: IE₁ + IE₂; double ΔatH(X) and EA for X₂.
  • EA₂(O) is endothermic (+798) — O²⁻ only survives inside a huge lattice.
  • Energy diagram: gaseous ions at the top; endothermic arrows up, exothermic down.
🔬 Covalency checklist (12.14–12.15)
  • Theoretical LE: perfect ionic model — spherical ions, purely electrostatic.
  • Experimental LE (Born–Haber) of greater magnitude ⇒ covalent character.
  • Mechanism: small, highly charged cation polarises a large anion → electron cloud distortion → sharing.
  • Gap grows: bigger anion (F⁻ → I⁻) and denser cation (Na⁺ → Ag⁺, Li⁺ → Mg²⁺).
  • Examples: NaCl 790 vs 787 (ionic) · LiI 759 vs 734 · AgCl 905 vs 770 (covalent).
💧 Solution cycles & solubility (12.16–12.19)

Cycle: ΔsolH = LE + ΔhydH(cation) + ΔhydH(anion). LE positive; hydrations negative.

Solubility test: ΔS_total(sol) = ΔS_sys + (−ΔsolH/T) > 0 ⇒ dissolving feasible.

  • Smaller/higher-charged ions ⇒ larger LE and more exothermic ΔhydH.
  • Group 2 hydroxides: more soluble down the group (lattice term dominates the change).
  • Group 2 sulfates: less soluble down the group (hydration term dominates the change).
⚠️ Master trap checklist (last-minute)
  • ΔH in J whenever it meets T or a J-valued ΔS — the topic’s #1 killer.
  • Feasibility needs ΔS_total > 0 — never judge by ΔS_sys or ΔH alone.
  • Feasible ≠ fast. Diamond: thermodynamically unstable, kinetically stable.
  • ΔS sign: count gas moles first; solids/liquids barely count.
  • S° of elements is NOT zero (only perfect crystals at 0 K).
  • Born–Haber: double ΔatH and EA for X₂; IE₁ + IE₂ for 2+ metals; EA₂(O) is +.
  • LE convention: IAL uses endothermic dissociation (+) — check the question’s definition.
  • Covalency: cation polarises anion (never reversed); gap = covalent character.
  • ΔhydH always exothermic; ΔsolH can be either sign; +ΔsolH ≠ insoluble.
  • Group 2: hydroxides ↑ solubility down; sulfates ↓.
