Essential learning goals and examiner watch-outs across amines, amides, amino acids, proteins, polymers and azo dyes.

Spec 19.1 Nomenclature

1. Naming Amines, Amides & Amino Acids

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Amines: name the alkyl group + "amine" (butylamine) or use amino- as a substituent; classify 1°/2°/3° by the number of carbon groups on N.
  • Amides: suffix -amide; $N$-substituents prefixed (e.g. $N$-methylethanamide, $N,N$-dimethylethanamide).
  • Amino acids: 2-aminopropanoic acid (alanine) — the $\alpha$-carbon bears both $-\text{NH}_2$ and $-\text{COOH}$.
  • Draw structural, displayed and skeletal formulae for each family.
  • Distinguish 1°/2°/3° for both amines and amides (count C groups on N).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Amide classification is by the number of carbon groups on nitrogen — $\text{CH}_3\text{CONH}_2$ is a primary amide even though N has two H. Use the $N$- prefix for substituents on nitrogen.

Spec 19.2 i – ii Amines as bases

2. Amines + Water and + Acids

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • With water: $\text{RNH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightleftharpoons \text{RNH}_3^+ + \text{OH}^-$ → alkaline solution (use reversible arrows).
  • Aliphatic amines give pH ≈ 11–12; phenylamine only ≈ 8 (weaker base).
  • With acids: form alkylammonium salts, e.g. $\text{RNH}_2 + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{RNH}_3^+\text{Cl}^-$.
  • Adding NaOH to the salt regenerates the free amine (insoluble oil for phenylamine).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

With $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$ the ratio is 2:1 (diprotic acid): $2\text{RNH}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow (\text{RNH}_3)_2\text{SO}_4$. This mole-ratio point is a classic trap.

Spec 19.2 iii – iv Amines as nucleophiles

3. Amines + Halogenoalkanes & + Ethanoyl Chloride

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • With halogenoalkanes (nucleophilic substitution): excess $\text{NH}_3$ gives the 1° amine; excess RX gives a mixture up to the quaternary salt.
  • Conditions: ethanol solvent, sealed tube / heat.
  • With ethanoyl chloride: nucleophilic addition–elimination → an amide + HCl.
  • 1° amine → $N$-substituted amide; 2° amine → $N,N$-disubstituted amide.
  • 3° amines do not react with acyl chlorides (no N–H to eliminate).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The acyl chloride + amine reaction needs 2 mol of amine (one to acylate, one to mop up the HCl). See the Amine Reactions matrix for the full equations.

Spec 19.2 v Amine + Cu²⁺

4. Amines + Copper(II) Ions — Base then Ligand

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Dropwise amine acts as a base: pale-blue $\text{Cu(OH)}_2(\text{H}_2\text{O})_4$ precipitate forms.
  • Excess amine acts as a ligand: the precipitate dissolves to a deep royal-blue complex.
  • Only 4 amine molecules coordinate: $[\text{Cu(RNH}_2)_4(\text{H}_2\text{O})_2]^{2+}$.
  • Recognise this as ligand exchange (a transition-metal reaction).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

State the two roles of the amine (base dropwise, ligand in excess) and the colour change pale blue ppt → deep blue solution. This links Topic 19 to Topic 17.

Spec 19.3 Basicity & solubility

5. Why Basicity Differs — NH₃ vs Aliphatic vs Aromatic

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Basicity order: aliphatic amine > $\text{NH}_3$ > phenylamine.
  • Alkyl groups are electron-releasing (+I) → raise electron density on N → lone pair more available.
  • In phenylamine the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring → less available → weaker base.
  • Small amines are miscible with water via H-bonding (N–H⋯O–H).
  • Solubility falls as the hydrophobic carbon chain lengthens.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

For full marks say the lone pair is "delocalised into the benzene ring / less available to accept a proton". Try the interactive Basicity Lab to visualise lone-pair availability.

Spec 19.4 Making aliphatic amines

6. Preparing Primary Aliphatic Amines

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • From halogenoalkanes: $\text{RX} + 2\text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{RNH}_2 + \text{NH}_4\text{X}$ (excess $\text{NH}_3$, ethanol, sealed tube).
  • Excess ammonia suppresses further substitution to 2°/3° amines.
  • From nitriles: reduce $\text{RCN}$ with $\text{LiAlH}_4$ (dry ether) or $\text{H}_2$/Ni → $\text{RCH}_2\text{NH}_2$.
  • The nitrile route adds one carbon to the chain.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Explain why excess ammonia (to favour the 1° amine) and why ethanol (to stop water competing as a nucleophile). Both are 1-mark explanations.

Spec 19.5 Making aromatic amines

7. Preparing Phenylamine — Nitro Reduction

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Reduce nitrobenzene with tin (Sn) + concentrated HCl, heat under reflux.
  • The product is the phenylammonium salt, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_3^+\text{Cl}^-$.
  • Add concentrated NaOH to liberate the free phenylamine (insoluble oil).
  • Overall: $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NO}_2 + 6[\text{H}] \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

You must include the NaOH step — without it you have the salt, not the amine. Examiners ask "why add NaOH?" (to free the amine from its salt).

Spec 19.6 Diazonium & azo dyes

8. Diazotisation & Azo Coupling

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Diazotisation: phenylamine + $\text{NaNO}_2$ + HCl, 0–10 °C → benzenediazonium chloride.
  • $\text{HNO}_2$ is generated in situ; above 10 °C the diazonium ion decomposes to phenol + $\text{N}_2$.
  • Coupling: diazonium ion + phenol in cold alkaline solution → orange/red azo dye.
  • The $-\text{N}=\text{N}-$ azo group links two aromatic rings (extended conjugation → colour).
  • Phenol is dissolved in NaOH to form the more reactive phenoxide ion.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The temperature control (0–10 °C) is the key condition — state it and the reason (diazonium decomposes if warmer). See the Azo Dyes tab for the full mechanism. Reagent: NaNO₂ + HCl in situ makes HNO₂ → NO⁺ that diazotises; HCl + HNO₃ instead gives nitration (NO₂⁺), not diazonium (WCH15 Jun 2026 Q11c).

Spec 19.7 Amide preparation

9. Making Amides from Acyl Chlorides

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Acyl chloride + conc. $\text{NH}_3$ → 1° amide: $\text{RCOCl} + 2\text{NH}_3 \rightarrow \text{RCONH}_2 + \text{NH}_4\text{Cl}$.
  • Acyl chloride + 1° amine → 2° ($N$-substituted) amide; + 2° amine → 3° amide.
  • Mechanism: nucleophilic addition–elimination; 2:1 amine:acyl chloride ratio.
  • 3° amines cannot form amides (no N–H to eliminate).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The 2:1 ratio (second amine neutralises the HCl) and the 3°-amine exception are the two most-tested points. The paracetamol synthesis is a favourite context.

Spec 19.8 – 19.9 Polymers

10. Condensation & Addition Polymerisation

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Polyamides (nylon 6,6, proteins): dicarboxylic acid + diamine, eliminating water; the amide link is $-\text{CONH}-$.
  • Addition polymers: poly(propenamide) from $\text{CH}_2=\text{CHCONH}_2$; the C=C opens, side group stays.
  • Poly(ethenol) is made in 2 steps (ethenol itself is unstable → tautomerises to ethanal).
  • Draw repeat units for all the polymers in 19.8.
  • Two-monomer condensation eliminates $2n\text{H}_2\text{O}$; single-monomer eliminates $n\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Know why poly(ethenol) needs 2 steps and draw its repeat unit correctly ($-\text{CH}_2\text{CH(OH)}-$). The $n\text{H}_2\text{O}$ vs $2n\text{H}_2\text{O}$ distinction is a common trap.

Spec 19.10 Polymer properties

11. Polyamide Strength & Poly(ethenol) Solubility

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Polyamides are strong (high tensile strength) due to H-bonding between C=O and N–H of adjacent chains.
  • Kevlar's para-substituted benzene rings give linear chains that pack tightly.
  • Poly(ethenol) is water-soluble because its –OH groups H-bond with water.
  • Applications: soluble hospital laundry bags and liquid-detergent capsules (liquitabs).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Polyesters have dipole–dipole forces, not H-bonding between chains — so polyamides have higher melting points. This comparison is a standard 2-mark question.

Spec 19.11 Amino acids

12. Amino Acids — Zwitterions, Optical Activity & Peptides

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • A zwitterion, $^+\text{H}_3\text{N–CH(R)–COO}^-$, is a dipolar ion with net charge 0 (at the isoelectric point).
  • Low pH → cation ($-\text{NH}_3^+$, $-\text{COOH}$); high pH → anion ($-\text{NH}_2$, $-\text{COO}^-$).
  • High melting points come from strong ionic forces in the zwitterion lattice (not just H-bonding).
  • Amino acids are optically active (chiral $\alpha$-carbon) — they rotate plane-polarised light.
  • Peptide bonds ($-\text{CONH}-$) form by condensation; hydrolysis (acid or alkali, reflux) breaks them.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Draw the zwitterion with both charges. 2 amino acids give 2 dipeptides; 3 give 6 tripeptides ($n!$). Try the interactive zwitterion pH switcher in the Amino Acids tab. Paper cue: WCH15 Jun 2026 Q13 (histidine) — high m.p. = zwitterion, strong ionic bonds; chelation = N/O lone pair → dative bond to metal (Co²⁺), multidentate chelating agent (not "ammonia ligand"); alkylation uses CH₃Cl/Br/I, not a Grignard.

Spec 19.12 Core Practical 15

13. Core Practical 15 — Analysing Unknowns

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Use a sequence of tests to identify organic and inorganic unknowns.
  • Amino acids are colourless on TLC → spray ninhydrin and heat to reveal purple spots.
  • Calculate $R_f = \dfrac{\text{distance moved by spot}}{\text{distance moved by solvent}}$ and compare with data-book values.
  • Combine solubility, pH, and functional-group tests to deduce an unknown.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The 3-mark TLC protocol: spray ninhydrin + heat → measure distances → calculate $R_f$ and compare. State why heating is needed (to develop the colourless spots).

Why Are Amines Different Bases? — Lone-Pair Availability

Basicity depends on how available the nitrogen lone pair is to accept a proton. Pick a base and watch the lone pair — the brighter it glows, the more available it is.

Relative basicity (K_b)
📌 The rule to memorise

Basicity order: aliphatic amine > ammonia > phenylamine. Alkyl groups are electron-releasing (+I), raising electron density on N and making the lone pair more available. In phenylamine the lone pair is delocalised into the benzene ring, so it is far less available to accept a proton.

Peptide bond formation (condensation)
$\text{H}_2\text{N-CH(R}_1)\text{-COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{N-CH(R}_2)\text{-COOH}$
$\rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{N-CH(R}_1)\text{-}\underset{\text{peptide link}}{\text{CONH}}\text{-CH(R}_2)\text{-COOH} + \text{H}_2\text{O}$

The $-\text{COOH}$ of one amino acid and the $-\text{NH}_2$ of another eliminate water to form the amide (peptide) link $-\text{CONH}-$. This is how proteins are built.

Combinatorics: 2 different amino acids → 2 dipeptides (A-B and B-A). 3 different → 6 tripeptides ($3! = 6$). Order matters!
Peptide hydrolysis (breaking the bond)
Acidic: $\text{R-CONH-R'} + \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{H}^+ \xrightarrow{\text{reflux}} \text{R-COOH} + \text{R'NH}_3^+$
Alkaline: $\text{R-CONH-R'} + \text{OH}^- \xrightarrow{\text{reflux}} \text{R-COO}^- + \text{R'NH}_2$

Both need heat under reflux. Acid hydrolysis gives protonated amino acids (cation); alkaline hydrolysis gives carboxylate salts (anion). This is how proteins are broken down for analysis.

Optical activity: the $\alpha$-carbon is chiral (4 different groups), so amino acids rotate plane-polarised light — except glycine (R = H, not chiral).
Synthesis Flow · Spec 19.6

Making an Azo Dye — Diazotisation then Coupling

The two-step route from phenylamine to a bright dye. Step 1 must be kept cold; step 2 needs alkaline conditions.

STEP 1 Diazotisation
$\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{NH}_2 + \text{NaNO}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \xrightarrow{0-10^\circ\text{C}} [\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2]^+\text{Cl}^- + \text{NaCl} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$
  • $\text{HNO}_2$ (nitrous acid) is generated in situ from $\text{NaNO}_2 + \text{HCl}$.
  • Temperature must stay 0–10 °C (ice bath).
  • Product: benzenediazonium chloride.
⚠️ Why cold? Above 10 °C the diazonium ion decomposes: $[\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2]^+ + \text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{OH} + \text{N}_2\uparrow + \text{H}^+$ — you'd lose your reagent as phenol and nitrogen gas.
STEP 2 Coupling with phenol
$[\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2]^+ + \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}^- \xrightarrow{\text{cold, alkaline}} \text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{-N=N-C}_6\text{H}_4\text{OH}$
  • Phenol is dissolved in NaOH to form the phenoxide ion, $\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{O}^-$.
  • The phenoxide ring is more electron-rich → attacks the diazonium electrophile.
  • Product: an azo dye with the $-\text{N}=\text{N}-$ link.
Why coloured? The $-\text{N}=\text{N}-$ group links two rings into one large conjugated $\pi$-system, which absorbs visible light. More conjugation → deeper colour.

Methyl Orangesulphonated azo dye — red in acid, yellow in alkali
