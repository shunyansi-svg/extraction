Topic 16 Learning Points & Mark Scheme Secrets (Spec 16.1 – 16.19)

Essential learning goals and examiner watch-outs across Redox Equilibria — electrode potentials, electrochemical cells, feasibility and redox titrations.

Spec 16.1 Redox foundations

1. Oxidation & Reduction — Electrons and Oxidation Numbers

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Oxidation = loss of electrons / increase in oxidation number; reduction = gain of electrons / decrease.
  • Assign oxidation numbers to s-, p- and d-block species (e.g. Mn in $\text{MnO}_4^-$ is +7).
  • Identify the oxidising agent (is reduced) and reducing agent (is oxidised).
  • Write and balance half-equations, including $\text{H}^+$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and electrons.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Balance half-equations fully: atoms first, then O with $\text{H}_2\text{O}$, H with $\text{H}^+$, then charge with $\text{e}^-$. The oxidising agent is the species that is reduced — students swap these constantly.

Spec 16.2 – 16.3 $E^\ominus$ & standard conditions

2. Standard Electrode Potential, $E^\ominus$

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • $E^\ominus$ = the emf of a half-cell compared with the standard hydrogen electrode under standard conditions.
  • Standard conditions: 298 K, 100 kPa (gases), 1.00 mol dm⁻³ (ions).
  • More positive $E^\ominus$ = greater tendency to be reduced (stronger oxidising agent).
  • More negative $E^\ominus$ = greater tendency to be oxidised (stronger reducing agent).
  • $E^\ominus$ values are quoted as reduction potentials (IUPAC convention).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

You must list all three standard conditions for full marks. $E^\ominus$ is an intensive property — multiplying the equation does NOT change $E^\ominus$.

Spec 16.4 Standard hydrogen electrode

3. The Standard Hydrogen Electrode (SHE)

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • SHE: platinum electrode in contact with $\text{H}^+(aq)$ at 1.00 mol dm⁻³ and $\text{H}_2(g)$ at 100 kPa, 298 K.
  • By definition $E^\ominus(\text{SHE}) = 0.00\ \text{V}$.
  • Platinum is used because it is inert and its porous/platinised surface catalyses the $\text{H}_2/\text{H}^+$ equilibrium.
  • A reference electrode is needed because a single half-cell's potential cannot be measured alone — only a difference between two.
  • Half-reaction: $2\text{H}^+(aq) + 2\text{e}^- \rightleftharpoons \text{H}_2(g)$.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

State platinum (not carbon) and give the reason (inert + catalytic surface). See the annotated SHE diagram in the SHE & Conditions tab.

Spec 16.5 – 16.6 Measuring $E^\ominus$

4. Measuring Standard Electrode Potentials

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Metal/ion half-cell: the metal dips into a 1.00 mol dm⁻³ solution of its own ions, connected to the SHE.
  • Ion/ion half-cell (e.g. $\text{Fe}^{3+}/\text{Fe}^{2+}$): an inert Pt electrode dips into a solution containing both ions.
  • The measured emf of the cell (vs SHE) is the $E^\ominus$ of the half-cell.
  • Core Practical 12: build and investigate simple electrochemical cells.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

For ion/ion systems there is no solid metal of the element, so an inert conductor (Pt) is essential — a common exam point.

Spec 16.7 Calculating $E^\ominus_{cell}$

5. Calculating the Standard Cell emf

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • $E^\ominus_{cell} = E^\ominus_{\text{reduction}} - E^\ominus_{\text{oxidation}} = E^\ominus_{\text{cathode}} - E^\ominus_{\text{anode}} = E^\ominus_{RHS} - E^\ominus_{LHS}$.
  • The half-cell with the more positive $E^\ominus$ undergoes reduction (cathode, RHS).
  • The half-cell with the more negative $E^\ominus$ undergoes oxidation (anode, LHS).
  • Do NOT multiply $E^\ominus$ by coefficients when balancing electrons.
  • A positive $E^\ominus_{cell}$ means the cell reaction is feasible as written.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The full step-by-step method is on the Calculate E° tab. The classic error is multiplying $E^\ominus$ by the balancing number — $E^\ominus$ never scales.

Spec 16.8 Cell diagrams

6. Writing Cell Diagrams (Conventional Representation)

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Convention: oxidation (anode) on the LEFT, reduction (cathode) on the RIGHT.
  • Single vertical line $|$ = phase boundary; double line $\parallel$ = salt bridge.
  • Write the oxidised form nearest the salt bridge: e.g. $\text{Zn}(s)\,|\,\text{Zn}^{2+}(aq)\,\parallel\,\text{Cu}^{2+}(aq)\,|\,\text{Cu}(s)$.
  • For ion/ion half-cells include the inert Pt electrode at the outer end: $\text{Pt}\,|\,\text{Fe}^{2+},\text{Fe}^{3+}\,\parallel\,\cdots$
  • Commas separate species in the same phase.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Practise assembling diagrams token-by-token in the Cell Diagrams tab. Getting LHS/RHS the wrong way round, or omitting Pt, are the top lost marks. In an electrochemical cell the species that is oxidised sits at the negative electrode (anode, LHS) — don't confuse it with the cathode. Paper cue: WCH15 Jun 2026 Q1 — the species oxidised is at the negative electrode, not the cathode.

Spec 16.9 Why conditions matter

7. Importance of Conditions When Measuring $E$

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Electrode potentials depend on concentration, temperature and pressure (Le Chatelier on the half-cell equilibrium).
  • Changing $[\text{ion}]$ shifts the half-cell equilibrium and changes $E$ from $E^\ominus$.
  • Standard conditions fix these so values are comparable and reproducible.
  • Increasing the concentration of a species being reduced makes $E$ more positive.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Explain via Le Chatelier: e.g. raising $[\text{Cu}^{2+}]$ drives $\text{Cu}^{2+}+2\text{e}^-\rightleftharpoons\text{Cu}$ right, making $E$ more positive. Try the concentration slider in The Cell tab.

Spec 16.10 – 16.11 Feasibility & thermodynamics

8. Predicting Feasibility & the Thermodynamic Link

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • A reaction is thermodynamically feasible if $E^\ominus_{cell} > 0$.
  • $E^\ominus_{cell}$ is directly proportional to $\Delta S_{total}$: $\Delta S_{total} = \dfrac{nFE^\ominus_{cell}}{T}$.
  • $E^\ominus_{cell}$ is directly proportional to $\ln K$: $\ln K = \dfrac{nFE^\ominus_{cell}}{RT}$.
  • Positive $E^\ominus_{cell}$ → positive $\Delta S_{total}$ → $K > 1$ → products favoured.
  • Given a set of half-reactions and $E^\ominus$ values, combine them to test feasibility.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Use the Feasibility tab to test any two systems from the data booklet. State "$E^\ominus_{cell}$ is positive, so the reaction is feasible" — and remember it says nothing about rate.

Spec 16.12 Limitations of $E^\ominus$ predictions

9. Limitations of $E^\ominus$ Predictions

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Kinetic stability: a feasible reaction ($E^\ominus_{cell}>0$) may be extremely slow due to a high activation energy.
  • Non-standard conditions: real concentrations/pressures differ from 1.00 mol dm⁻³ / 100 kPa, shifting $E$.
  • So a predicted-feasible reaction may not be observed, and a predicted-non-feasible one may occur under non-standard conditions.
  • Give examples (e.g. concentrated acid changing the outcome of $\text{MnO}_4^-$ reductions).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

"Thermodynamically feasible but kinetically stable" is the phrase examiners want. Always mention both limitations for full marks.

Spec 16.13 Electrochemical series

10. The Electrochemical Series

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Standard electrode potentials are reduction potentials, listed from most negative to most positive.
  • Top (most negative) = strongest reducing agents (easily oxidised).
  • Bottom (most positive) = strongest oxidising agents (easily reduced).
  • A species higher up reduces the oxidised form of any species below it.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

The data booklet lists all 31 systems as reductions. Use the series to quickly spot which species is reduced and which oxidised.

Spec 16.14 Disproportionation

11. Disproportionation Reactions

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • Disproportionation: the same element is simultaneously oxidised and reduced.
  • Test feasibility by combining the element's two relevant half-reactions.
  • Example: $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$; $E^\ominus_{cell} = +1.77 - (+0.68) = +1.09\ \text{V}$ (feasible).
  • Oxygen in $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ (−1) is both oxidised to 0 ($\text{O}_2$) and reduced to −2 ($\text{H}_2\text{O}$).
  • Use $E^\ominus$ values to predict whether a species will disproportionate.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Show the oxidation numbers changing in both directions. The Disprop & Applications tab has a step-by-step $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ worked example and a predictor. Paper cue: WCH15 Jun 2026 Q16(b) — Mn disproportionation: 3MnO₄³⁻ + 4H₂O → 2MnO₂ + MnO₄⁻ + 8OH⁻; Ecell = 0.93 − 0.27 = +0.66 V, positive → feasible. Penalise uncancelled electrons in the balanced equation.

Spec 16.15 – 16.17 Redox titrations

12. Redox Titrations — $\text{MnO}_4^-$/Fe²⁺ and Iodine/Thiosulfate

🎯 6 Key Learning Goals:
  • $\text{MnO}_4^- + 8\text{H}^+ + 5\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Mn}^{2+} + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$ (self-indicating: purple → colourless).
  • $\text{MnO}_4^-$ : Fe²⁺ ratio is 1 : 5.
  • Iodine/thiosulfate: $\text{I}_2 + 2\text{S}_2\text{O}_3^{2-} \rightarrow 2\text{I}^- + \text{S}_4\text{O}_6^{2-}$ (1 : 2), starch indicator (blue-black → colourless).
  • Carry out structured and unstructured titration calculations (moles → ratio → concentration/percentage).
  • Core Practicals 13a & 13b: perform both titrations.
  • Link to indirect determinations (e.g. % iron in tablets, % copper in brass).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Get the mole ratios right (1:5 and 1:2). Add starch only near the end-point of the iodine titration. $\text{MnO}_4^-$ needs acid ($\text{H}^+$) — state dilute $\text{H}_2\text{SO}_4$.

Spec 16.16 Uncertainty

13. Uncertainty & Validity of Results

🎯 4 Key Learning Goals:
  • Estimate the uncertainty of a measurement (e.g. burette ±0.05 cm³ per reading).
  • Calculate percentage uncertainty and combine them for a final result.
  • Discuss how uncertainty affects the validity of a calculated concentration/percentage.
  • Suggest improvements (repeat readings, more precise apparatus, larger titres).
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

A titre uses two burette readings, so the burette uncertainty is doubled. Show the % uncertainty working — it is usually worth 2 marks.

Spec 16.18 – 16.19 Fuel cells

14. Hydrogen–Oxygen Fuel Cells

🎯 5 Key Learning Goals:
  • A fuel cell converts the energy of fuel + oxygen reaction directly into a voltage.
  • Hydrogen-rich fuels (methanol, etc.) are used as well as pure $\text{H}_2$.
  • Acidic electrolyte: anode $\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^-$; cathode $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.
  • Alkaline electrolyte: anode $\text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2\text{e}^-$; cathode $\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$.
  • Advantages: high efficiency, only water produced; disadvantages: $\text{H}_2$ storage, cost, infrastructure.
💡 IAL Examiner Watch-Outs:

Know both the acidic and alkaline electrode reactions — examiners specify which electrolyte. Overall: $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$.

Related but covered elsewhere: the Nernst equation (quantitative non-standard potentials) is beyond the IAL spec — only the qualitative Le Chatelier effect is required. Electrolysis and industrial electrochemistry are not in this topic. The full fuel-cell economics discussion is in the Topic 16 revision hub.

The Method · Spec 16.7

Calculating $E^\ominus_{cell}$ — Step by Step

This is the calculation students find hardest. Follow the four steps in order, every time — the interactive below walks you through them.

Step 1

Write both $E^\ominus$ values exactly as given (they are reduction potentials).

Step 2

The more positive one is reduced (cathode). The other is reversed to oxidation (anode).

Step 3

$E^\ominus_{cell} = E^\ominus_{red} - E^\ominus_{ox}$. Do NOT multiply $E^\ominus$ by any coefficient.

Step 4

Balance the electrons and add the half-equations for the overall reaction.

Try it — pick any two half-cells from the data booklet
Worked example — the Daniell cell

Given $E^\ominus(\text{Zn}^{2+}/\text{Zn}) = -0.76\ \text{V}$ and $E^\ominus(\text{Cu}^{2+}/\text{Cu}) = +0.34\ \text{V}$, calculate $E^\ominus_{cell}$ and write the overall equation.

Step 2: Cu is more positive → Cu²⁺ reduced (cathode); Zn oxidised (anode).
Step 3: $E^\ominus_{cell} = +0.34 - (-0.76) = +1.10\ \text{V}$
Step 4: $\text{Zn} \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^-$ and $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$ (electrons already equal)
Overall: $\text{Zn}(s) + \text{Cu}^{2+}(aq) \rightarrow \text{Zn}^{2+}(aq) + \text{Cu}(s)$

⚠️ If the question gave you the Ag/Zn cell, you would still NOT double $E^\ominus(\text{Ag}^+/\text{Ag})$ even though you double the silver half-equation to balance electrons.

Animated Multi-Step Redox · Spec 16.10, 16.12

Vanadium Stepwise Reduction — Watch the Colours Change

Zinc ($E^\ominus = -0.76\ \text{V}$) reduces vanadium(V) one electron at a time. Each step has its own $E^\ominus_{cell}$ — keep adding zinc and see how far the reduction goes.

The four reduction steps (each with its own $E^\ominus_{cell}$)
💡 Why does the reduction stop at V²⁺?

The first three steps all have positive $E^\ominus_{cell}$ (+1.76, +1.10, +0.50 V), so they are feasible. But the fourth step, $\text{V}^{2+} + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow \text{V}$, has $E^\ominus_{cell} = -1.18 - (-0.76) = \mathbf{-0.42\ V}$ — negative, so zinc cannot reduce V²⁺ to vanadium metal. The reaction halts, leaving a violet V²⁺ solution. This is a classic exam question on using $E^\ominus$ to predict how far a reaction goes.

Reference Electrode · Spec 16.3 – 16.4, 16.9

The Standard Hydrogen Electrode

Every $E^\ominus$ is measured against this one electrode, defined as exactly 0.00 V.

Why a reference electrode is needed

You can never measure the potential of a single half-cell — a voltmeter only reads the difference between two. So we define one electrode as exactly 0.00 V (the SHE) and measure everything else against it.

Why platinum?

There is no solid metal in the $\text{H}^+/\text{H}_2$ system, so we need an inert conductor. Platinum is unreactive and its platinised (rough) surface catalyses the $\text{H}_2 \rightleftharpoons 2\text{H}^+$ equilibrium so it responds quickly.

The three standard conditions (learn these)
  • Temperature = 298 K (25 °C)
  • Gas pressure = 100 kPa (for $\text{H}_2$)
  • Ion concentration = 1.00 mol dm⁻³ (for $\text{H}^+$)
💡 Examiner watch-out

A common alternative is the calomel electrode — it doesn't need $\text{H}_2$ gas, which is awkward to handle. You may be asked why SHE is rarely used in school labs (hydrogen is flammable; the setup is fiddly).

Applications · Spec 16.14 – 16.19

Disproportionation, Fuel Cells & Redox Titrations

Disproportionation — worked example ($\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$)

Hydrogen peroxide contains oxygen in the −1 oxidation state, which can be both oxidised (to 0 in $\text{O}_2$) and reduced (to −2 in $\text{H}_2\text{O}$) — so it disproportionates.

Reduction: $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}\quad E^\ominus = +1.77\ \text{V}$
Oxidation (reversed): $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^-\quad E^\ominus = +0.68\ \text{V}$
$E^\ominus_{cell} = +1.77 - (+0.68) = +1.09\ \text{V} > 0 \Rightarrow$ feasible
Overall: $2\text{H}_2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2$

Yet $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$ sits on the shelf for months — a perfect example of thermodynamically feasible but kinetically stable (Spec 16.12). A catalyst (e.g. $\text{MnO}_2$) speeds it up.

Hydrogen–oxygen fuel cell — both electrolytes
Acidic electrolyte (H⁺)

Anode: $\text{H}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}^+ + 2\text{e}^-$

Cathode: $\text{O}_2 + 4\text{H}^+ + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$

Alkaline electrolyte (OH⁻)

Anode: $\text{H}_2 + 2\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 2\text{e}^-$

Cathode: $\text{O}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + 4\text{e}^- \rightarrow 4\text{OH}^-$

Overall (both): $2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$. Advantages: high efficiency, only water produced. Disadvantages: $\text{H}_2$ storage/transport, cost of Pt catalyst, lack of refuelling infrastructure.

Reference · Spec 16.13

The Electrochemical Series — All 31 Standard Electrode Potentials

Listed from most negative (strongest reducing agents, top) to most positive (strongest oxidising agents, bottom). A species higher up will reduce the oxidised form of any species below it.

Top of the series (most negative)

Strong reducing agents — they are easily oxidised (lose electrons). E.g. Na, Mg, Al, Zn. These sit at the top and push electrons out.

Bottom of the series (most positive)

Strong oxidising agents — they are easily reduced (gain electrons). E.g. $\text{MnO}_4^-$, $\text{H}_2\text{O}_2$, $\text{Cl}_2$. These sit at the bottom and pull electrons in.
